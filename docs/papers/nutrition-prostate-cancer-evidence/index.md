# Nutrition and Prostate Cancer: Evidence Map

Retrieved: **2026-09-26**  
Category: **Nutrition**  
Scope: diet, dietary patterns, body composition, metabolic health, and selected supplements before diagnosis, after diagnosis, during treatment, and after treatment/survivorship.

!!! warning "Not medical advice"
    This page summarizes evidence for education and clinician discussion. It does not recommend any diet, supplement, fasting program, or treatment choice. Nutrition changes should be discussed with qualified clinicians, especially during active treatment, weight loss, diabetes management, anticoagulant use, kidney disease, frailty, or when supplements may interact with surgery, radiation, ADT, chemotherapy, or other medicines.

## Nutrition topics covered

- **Dietary patterns**: Mediterranean, plant-based, healthy diet indices, Western/inflammatory patterns.
- **Single foods/nutrients**: dairy/calcium, whole milk vs low-fat/non-fat dairy, eggs/choline, red/processed meat, poultry with skin vs skinless poultry, tomato sauce/lycopene, fish/omega-3 and omega-6 balance.
- **Active surveillance interventions**: vegetable-rich diet counseling, omega-3/low omega-6 diet plus fish oil, weight-loss diet/exercise programs.
- **ADT/metabolic support**: low-carbohydrate diet plus walking, diet/exercise behavior programs.
- **Fasting mimicking diet / periodic fasting**: Prof Valter Longo's FMD framework, prostate-cancer pilot evidence, cancer-adjunct trials in other cancers, and safety caveats.
- **Protein / IGF-1 / mTOR questions**: high-protein and animal-protein hypotheses, ageing biology, prostate-cancer IGF evidence, and the countervailing need to preserve muscle during ADT and older age.
- **Survivorship diet quality**: diet after diagnosis, progression, recurrence, cancer-specific mortality, and all-cause mortality.

## Evidence synthesis by patient journey

### 1. Before diagnosis: incidence and risk of developing prostate cancer

The most patient-relevant message is that diet evidence is less definitive than claims often suggest. A 2025 systematic review of prospective cohorts and randomized trials found that among men without prostate cancer at baseline, plant-based and healthy dietary patterns were generally associated with lower total prostate cancer risk, while results varied across studies. A 2024 umbrella review emphasized that few modifiable risk factors are firmly established.

For specific dietary exposures, Western dietary patterns were associated with increased prostate cancer risk in a 2016 meta-analysis. Dairy and calcium showed modest positive associations with total prostate cancer risk in a 2015 cohort meta-analysis. Lycopene has long been studied, but a Cochrane review concluded evidence was insufficient for recommending lycopene supplementation for prostate cancer prevention.

**Interpretation:** a plant-forward, minimally processed, cardiometabolic-health diet is reasonable to discuss, but single-food or supplement claims should be treated cautiously.

### 2. After diagnosis: progression, recurrence, and mortality

Post-diagnosis evidence increasingly favors overall dietary patterns. A 2024 JAMA Network Open cohort found higher plant-based diet scores after diagnosis were associated with lower risk of disease progression among men with nonmetastatic prostate cancer. A 2021 active surveillance cohort found higher Mediterranean diet adherence was associated with lower risk of grade group progression.

Older Health Professionals Follow-Up Study data suggested post-diagnosis fish and tomato sauce intake were inversely associated with progression. More recent systematic reviews of post-diagnosis diet and lifestyle in prostate cancer survivors report that plant foods, healthier patterns, and minimizing inflammatory/hyperinsulinemic patterns are promising, but findings are not perfectly consistent.

**Key caution:** many post-diagnosis diet findings are observational. Men who eat healthier diets may also differ in healthcare access, exercise, weight, smoking, comorbidities, screening intensity, and treatment choices.

### 3. Active surveillance and treatment period

The best-known active surveillance RCT, MEAL, successfully increased vegetable intake but did **not** significantly reduce cancer progression over 24 months. This is important because it pushes against simplistic “eat more vegetables to stop progression” claims.

CAPFISH-3 tested a high omega-3, low omega-6 diet with fish oil in men on active surveillance and found a favorable change in Ki-67, a biopsy proliferation biomarker, over one year. This is promising but should not be overinterpreted as proof of better long-term cancer outcomes.

In overweight/obese men on active surveillance, PALS tested a structured diet and exercise weight-loss program and primarily targeted obesity/metabolic biomarkers rather than definitive prostate cancer outcomes. In men starting ADT, CAPS1 and IDEA-P suggest diet/lifestyle interventions can improve or support metabolic and functional outcomes, but these are supportive-care findings, not proven anti-cancer effects.

Prof Valter Longo's fasting mimicking diet (FMD) framework is relevant here because it proposes short, periodic, low-calorie/low-sugar/low-protein cycles designed to lower nutrient-signalling pathways such as insulin/IGF-1 and mTOR while being more practical than water-only fasting. The prostate-cancer-specific human evidence is still early: a small prospective implementation study of periodic 4-day FMD cycles in prostate cancer patients with metabolic features reported weight, waist, blood-pressure, glucose, cholesterol, and PSA changes, but it was not a randomized cancer-outcome trial. In other cancers, especially breast cancer neoadjuvant chemotherapy, randomized FMD evidence is more developed but remains **adjacent evidence**, not direct prostate-cancer proof.

Longo's protein hypothesis is also worth including, but cautiously. The ageing-biology argument is that high protein intake, especially animal protein in midlife, can increase IGF-1 and nutrient-sensing pathways including mTOR, which may accelerate ageing biology and plausibly support cancer growth. However, prostate cancer patients are often older and may be on ADT, where sarcopenia, frailty, bone loss, treatment tolerance, and falls risk are major competing hazards. Thus the patient question is not simply “low protein good, high protein bad”; it is how to balance plant-forward diet quality and insulin/IGF/mTOR hypotheses against adequate protein, resistance training, muscle preservation, kidney function, and treatment phase.

### 4. After treatment has ended: survivorship

Survivorship reviews suggest better overall diet quality and Mediterranean-style patterns may be associated with improved prognosis in cancer survivors, including prostate cancer, but prostate-specific intervention evidence remains limited. Nutrition after treatment should be framed around recovery, cardiometabolic health, bowel health, muscle preservation, weight stability or safe weight loss, and supporting tolerance of ongoing therapies if any.

**Interpretation:** after treatment, nutrition is best treated as survivorship support and risk-factor management rather than as a substitute for surveillance or clinician-directed treatment.

### UCSF video appraisal: post-treatment diet advice, dairy, eggs, and meat

**Source reviewed:** Stacey Kenfield, PhD, UCSF/UCTV 2026 talk, [*Prostate Cancer: Diet and Exercise*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l1yqcfhutvo&t=882s). The UCTV page and transcript identify the talk as a 2026 UCSF presentation on diet and exercise for prostate cancer patients.

**Bottom line for the website:** the video is **more nuanced than a blanket “avoid all dairy, eggs, and meat” message**. It recommends a mostly plant-based eating pattern, but explicitly says plant-based does not mean everything must be plants and that smaller amounts of meat, dairy, eggs, fish, and seafood can still be included. Its strongest cautions are about **processed meat, saturated fat, whole milk, high egg intake, and poultry with skin**. That is broadly consistent with this website's cautious plant-forward/Mediterranean framing, but the site should be clearer about these specific foods.

| UCSF video claim | Evidence-based interpretation for this library | Practical wording |
|---|---|---|
| Exercise has the most consistent post-diagnosis evidence | Agrees with the Exercise evidence map; diet evidence is more mixed and observational. | Prioritize exercise plus diet quality rather than diet alone. |
| Adopt a plant-based eating pattern | Consistent with 2024-2026 cohort/systematic-review evidence associating plant-forward/Mediterranean/lower-inflammatory patterns with lower progression risk. | “Mostly plant foods” is a reasonable target; it is not the same as mandatory veganism. |
| Limit processed meat and saturated fat | Supported by diet-pattern evidence and survivorship reviews; processed red meat evidence after diagnosis remains limited but concerning enough to minimize. | Avoid or minimize processed meats; make saturated fat reduction a cardiometabolic and possible prostate-cancer-risk discussion. |
| Whole milk is a consistent concern | Direct post-diagnosis cohort data link whole milk, not low-fat/non-fat dairy overall, with recurrence/lethal progression signals. | Prefer non-dairy calcium sources or low-fat/non-fat dairy if dairy is used; avoid high whole-milk intake. |
| Low-fat dairy is not consistently associated with progression/lethal prostate cancer | This distinction is important and prevents overreading “dairy” as all dairy in all contexts. | Do not state that all dairy must be eliminated; discuss calcium/bone health, especially with ADT. |
| Eggs: very high intake may be a concern | New systematic review evidence says egg findings are inconsistent: one study signaled risk at about 5.5 servings/week, while several studies with lower highest categories did not. | A cautious target such as fewer than 3 whole eggs/week is defensible for discussion, but not proven as a required threshold. |
| Protein is still needed in older adults | This aligns with the site's Longo/FMD caution: older men, men on ADT, and men doing resistance training need to avoid sarcopenia/frailty. | Shift protein sources toward beans/lentils, soy foods, nuts/seeds, fish, skinless poultry, and possibly low-fat dairy; do not apply a universal low-protein rule. |

**Strengths of the video**

- It starts by saying diet evidence is complicated, sometimes conflicting, and less consistent than exercise evidence.
- It uses a patient-relevant survivorship frame: many men die with prostate cancer rather than from it, so cardiovascular/metabolic health matters.
- It distinguishes whole milk from low-fat dairy, and high egg intake from lower intake, rather than treating all dairy or all eggs as equal.
- It recommends dietitian support, calcium planning, and adequate protein for older adults rather than unsupervised restriction.

**Weaknesses / caveats**

- The talk is short and time-limited, so some recommendations are compressed and could sound more absolute than the underlying observational evidence allows.
- Much of the food-specific evidence comes from cohort studies, not randomized trials powered for metastasis, prostate-cancer mortality, or overall survival.
- “Avoid/limit” tables can blur different evidence levels: processed meat, whole milk, eggs, saturated fat, and poultry with skin do not all have the same consistency or endpoint support.
- The calcium message needs individualization: men on ADT may need bone-health planning, and calcium can come from low-fat dairy, fortified foods, low-oxalate greens, tofu, beans, almonds, canned fish, or supplements when clinically appropriate.

**Website update from this appraisal:** keep the overall plant-forward/Mediterranean framing, but add more explicit food-specific cautions: avoid/minimize processed meat; avoid high whole-milk intake; do not overstate low-fat/non-fat dairy risk; keep whole eggs modest, especially avoiding high intake; choose skinless poultry/fish/plant proteins over poultry with skin and processed/red meat where practical.

### Deep dive: the case against whole/full-cream milk after prostate cancer diagnosis

**Why this gets a separate note:** “avoid dairy” is too broad. The strongest post-diagnosis signal is narrower: **whole/full-fat milk** or high-fat dairy patterns, while low-fat/non-fat dairy is not consistently associated with worse prostate-cancer outcomes. This matters because men on ADT may also need a bone-health plan, calcium/vitamin D review, and adequate protein.

#### Direct post-diagnosis studies

| Study | Population and endpoint | Whole/full-fat milk result | What it does and does not prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pettersson et al. 2012, HPFS** | 3,918 men with apparently localized prostate cancer; metastases or prostate-cancer death | Total milk/dairy after diagnosis were not associated with lethal prostate cancer, but highest whole-milk intake was associated with higher progression/lethal risk; low-fat dairy was not associated with increased risk. | Supports a specific caution about whole milk, not a blanket anti-dairy conclusion. |
| **Yang et al. 2015, Physicians' Health Study** | 926 men with nonmetastatic prostate cancer; prostate-cancer-specific and all-cause mortality | ≥3 dairy servings/day after diagnosis was associated with higher all-cause mortality and a higher prostate-cancer-specific mortality estimate; high-fat dairy appeared stronger than low-fat dairy, but the difference was not statistically significant. | Supports concern about high dairy/high-fat dairy intake, but is less specific to whole milk and has wide prostate-cancer mortality confidence intervals. |
| **Tat et al. 2018, CaPSURE** | 1,334 men with nonmetastatic prostate cancer; recurrence composite | >4 servings/week of whole milk vs 0-3 servings/month was associated with higher recurrence risk; the association was stronger among men with BMI ≥27 kg/m²; low-fat milk and other dairy foods were not associated with recurrence. | The clearest direct reason to minimize whole/full-fat milk after diagnosis; still observational. |
| **Song et al. 2013, Physicians' Health Study** | 21,660 male physicians plus survival analysis among 2,806 prostate-cancer cases | Whole milk, not skim/low-fat milk, was consistently associated with fatal prostate cancer and progression to fatal disease among cases. | Supportive but partly prediagnostic/baseline exposure, so it is less direct than Pettersson/Tat for post-diagnosis choices. |

#### Meta-analysis and review context

- **Aune et al. 2015** meta-analyzed cohort studies of dairy/calcium and prostate cancer incidence. It found modest positive associations for total dairy, milk, low-fat milk, cheese, dietary calcium, and prostate cancer risk, but this mainly addresses **developing** prostate cancer rather than outcomes after diagnosis.
- **UCSF/PCF-style survivorship reviews** and the 2026 postdiagnosis diet/lifestyle review support plant-forward, lower-inflammatory, cardiometabolic-health dietary patterns, but they do not provide randomized proof that removing whole milk reduces recurrence or metastasis.
- There is no clean randomized trial or high-quality meta-analysis specifically proving that **post-diagnosis whole milk avoidance** improves prostate-cancer survival. The evidence is a consistent-enough cohort signal for caution, not causal proof.

#### Evidence strength assessment

| Claim | Strength | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Avoiding or minimizing **whole/full-fat milk** after diagnosis is reasonable | **Moderate-low to moderate** | Supported by repeated prospective cohort signals and plausible mechanisms, but no randomized outcome trial. |
| Whole milk **causes** recurrence or prostate-cancer death | **Not established** | Cohort associations may be confounded by broader diet, BMI, activity, screening, comorbidities, or treatment differences. |
| All dairy should be eliminated after diagnosis | **Weak** | Direct post-diagnosis studies distinguish whole milk from low-fat/non-fat dairy; total dairy findings are mixed. |
| Calcium and protein needs can be ignored because of prostate risk | **Incorrect / unsafe** | ADT, ageing, frailty, osteoporosis, kidney disease, and sarcopenia risk require individualized planning. |

#### Proposed mechanisms

These mechanisms are biologically plausible, but none has been proven as the causal pathway for the whole-milk cohort signal:

1. **Saturated fat / high-fat dairy pattern:** whole milk may contribute to saturated fat intake, weight gain, insulin resistance, inflammation, and adverse lipid/cardiovascular risk. This may be especially relevant during ADT, which can worsen fat mass, insulin resistance, lipids, and cardiovascular risk.
2. **IGF-1 signalling:** milk and dairy protein can raise circulating IGF-I modestly, and higher IGF-I is associated with prostate cancer risk in large pooled analyses. IGF-I can promote proliferation, survival signalling, and insulin/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activity. This is plausible biology, but it does not prove that stopping whole milk after diagnosis improves outcomes.
3. **Calcium/vitamin D hypothesis:** high calcium intake has been hypothesized to suppress active vitamin D, which may reduce anti-proliferative signalling in prostate cells. This is a possible pathway but does not map perfectly onto the post-diagnosis evidence because low-fat dairy also contains calcium and is not consistently adverse.
4. **Body-composition interaction:** the CaPSURE whole-milk association was stronger in men with BMI ≥27 kg/m², suggesting that adiposity, insulin resistance, inflammation, or broader metabolic state may modify risk. BMI is crude, so DEXA/body composition and cardiometabolic markers may be more useful clinically.
5. **Marker of Western diet:** whole milk can be a marker for a wider dietary pattern high in saturated fat, processed/red meat, refined grains, and lower plant-food intake. Statistical adjustment cannot fully remove this possibility.

#### Patient-facing interpretation

The most balanced conclusion is: **avoid or minimize whole/full-cream milk after prostate cancer diagnosis**, especially if overweight, metabolically unhealthy, or on ADT. If dairy is used, discuss low-fat/non-fat dairy versus non-dairy calcium/protein sources with a clinician or dietitian. Do not turn this into unsupervised calcium restriction, protein restriction, or a claim that all dairy is proven harmful.

#### Clinician discussion prompts

- Does my treatment plan include ADT or another reason to assess bone density, vitamin D, calcium intake, and fracture risk?
- Should I avoid whole/full-cream milk specifically while using low-fat/non-fat dairy or fortified non-dairy alternatives if needed?
- If I remove whole milk, what replaces it nutritionally: calcium-fortified soy milk, tofu set with calcium, low-oxalate greens, beans, almonds, canned fish with bones, or supplements?
- Is my priority prostate-cancer risk reduction, cardiovascular risk reduction, bone protection, muscle preservation, weight management, or all of these?
- Should I see an oncology dietitian before making major dairy, protein, fasting, or weight-loss changes?

### After diagnosis: progression, recurrence, and mortality

#### Diet after diagnosis and the risk of prostate cancer progression, recurrence, and death

- **Citation:** Chan JM, Holick CN, Leitzmann MF, et al. *Cancer Causes & Control*. 2006.
- **DOI/PMID:** DOI [10.1007/s10552-005-0413-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10552-005-0413-4); PMID [16425098](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16425098/)
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16425098/
- **Date:** 2006 Mar
- **Category:** Nutrition; Active Surveillance; Side Effects & Quality of Life
- **Evidence type:** Prospective cohort study after diagnosis.
- **Population:** 1,202 men with localized/regional prostate cancer in the Health Professionals Follow-Up Study.
- **Patient relevance:** Directly studies diet after diagnosis and progression outcomes.
- **Main finding:** Higher fish and tomato sauce consumption were inversely associated with prostate cancer progression in this cohort.
- **Limitations:** Observational, older treatment/surveillance context, self-reported diet, possible confounding.
- **Questions for clinician:** Are fish and tomato-rich foods reasonable within a broader cardiometabolic diet plan?

#### Postdiagnostic red meat, poultry, and egg intake and prostate cancer outcomes — systematic review

- **Citation:** Sandhu S, Teshome H, Van Blarigan EL, Chan JM, et al. *Journal of Urology*. 2026.
- **DOI/PMID:** DOI [10.1097/JU.0000000000005263](https://doi.org/10.1097/JU.0000000000005263); PMID [42607142](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42607142/)
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42607142/
- **Date:** 2026
- **Category:** Nutrition; Survivorship; Side Effects & Quality of Life
- **Evidence type:** Systematic review of prospective cohort studies after prostate cancer diagnosis.
- **Population:** Six cohort studies including more than 10,000 men with prostate cancer.
- **Patient relevance:** Directly addresses Terry's question about red meat, poultry, and eggs after diagnosis.
- **Main finding:** Evidence was limited and inconsistent. Total red meat was not associated with progression outcomes across five studies, but processed red meat and all-cause mortality/prostate-cancer-specific signals remain concerning. Poultry with skin appeared riskier than skinless poultry. Egg evidence was inconsistent: a positive progression association appeared in one study with very high intake, while several studies with lower highest-intake groups did not show associations.
- **Limitations:** Cohort evidence, self-reported diet, varied endpoints and intake categories; not a randomized diet prescription trial.
- **Questions for clinician:** Should I minimize processed meat, avoid poultry skin, keep whole eggs modest, and replace red/processed meat with fish, legumes, soy foods, nuts/seeds, or skinless poultry while maintaining adequate protein?

#### Milk and other dairy foods after diagnosis: CaPSURE recurrence cohort

- **Citation:** Tat D, Kenfield SA, Cowan JE, et al. *The Prostate*. 2018.
- **DOI/PMID:** DOI [10.1002/pros.23441](https://doi.org/10.1002/pros.23441); PMID [29105845](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29105845/)
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29105845/
- **Date:** 2018 Jan
- **Category:** Nutrition; Survivorship
- **Evidence type:** Prospective cohort after diagnosis.
- **Population:** 1,334 men with nonmetastatic prostate cancer in CaPSURE.
- **Patient relevance:** Directly informs the UCSF video's distinction between whole milk and low-fat/non-fat dairy.

#### Milk and dairy consumption and lethal prostate cancer in HPFS

- **Citation:** Pettersson A, Kasperzyk JL, Kenfield SA, et al. *Cancer Epidemiology, Biomarkers & Prevention*. 2012.
- **DOI/PMID:** DOI [10.1158/1055-9965.EPI-11-1004](https://doi.org/10.1158/1055-9965.EPI-11-1004); PMID [22315365](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22315365/)
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22315365/
- **Date:** 2012 Mar
- **Category:** Nutrition; Survivorship
- **Evidence type:** Prospective cohort after diagnosis.
- **Population:** 3,918 men diagnosed with apparently localized prostate cancer in the Health Professionals Follow-Up Study.
- **Patient relevance:** Supports caution about whole milk while avoiding a blanket claim that all dairy after diagnosis is harmful.
- **Main finding:** Total milk and dairy intake after diagnosis were not associated with greater lethal prostate cancer risk, but the highest whole-milk intake was associated with increased progression risk; low-fat dairy was not associated with increased risk.
- **Limitations:** Observational evidence; diet and prostate cancer treatment contexts changed over time.
- **Questions for clinician:** How should dairy choices be balanced against calcium/vitamin D needs, ADT bone risk, cardiovascular risk, and personal dietary preferences?

#### Dairy intake after prostate cancer diagnosis and mortality: Physicians' Health Study

- **Citation:** Yang M, Kenfield SA, Van Blarigan EL, et al. *International Journal of Cancer*. 2015.
- **DOI/PMID:** DOI [10.1002/ijc.29608](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.29608); PMID [25989745](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25989745/)
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25989745/
- **Date:** 2015 Nov 15
- **Category:** Nutrition; Survivorship
- **Evidence type:** Prospective cohort after diagnosis.
- **Population:** 926 men in the Physicians' Health Study with nonmetastatic prostate cancer who completed diet questionnaires after diagnosis.
- **Patient relevance:** Adds mortality context to the whole-milk/high-fat dairy question.
- **Main finding:** Men consuming at least 3 servings/day of dairy after diagnosis had higher all-cause mortality and a higher prostate-cancer-specific mortality estimate than men consuming less than 1 serving/day; high-fat dairy appeared more strongly associated than low-fat dairy, but the high-fat versus low-fat difference was not statistically significant.
- **Limitations:** Observational, dietary questionnaire completed a median of 5 years after diagnosis, 56 prostate-cancer deaths, wide confidence intervals for disease-specific mortality, and possible confounding.
- **Questions for clinician:** Is high total dairy or high-fat dairy intake displacing plant foods, fish, legumes, or other cardiometabolic-health foods in my diet?

- **Population:** 21,660 US male physicians; survival analysis among 2,806 incident prostate cancer cases.
- **Patient relevance:** Helps explain why clinicians often distinguish whole milk from skim/low-fat milk.
- **Main finding:** Skim/low-fat milk was more associated with nonaggressive prostate cancer incidence, whereas whole milk was associated with fatal prostate cancer and with progression to fatal disease among prostate cancer cases.
- **Limitations:** Milk intake was collected at baseline, often before diagnosis; this is supportive but less direct for post-diagnosis dietary changes.
- **Questions for clinician:** Does the whole-milk signal justify switching to low-fat/non-fat or fortified non-dairy alternatives while maintaining bone-health nutrition?

#### Milk, IGF signalling, and prostate cancer: systematic review and meta-analysis

- **Citation:** Harrison S, Lennon R, Holly J, et al. *Cancer Causes & Control*. 2017.
- **DOI/PMID:** DOI [10.1007/s10552-017-0883-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10552-017-0883-1); PMID [28361446](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28361446/)
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28361446/
- **Date:** 2017 Jun
- **Category:** Nutrition; Mechanisms; Hormones
- **Evidence type:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of milk-IGF and IGF-prostate cancer evidence.
- **Population:** 172 included studies across milk/IGF, IGF/prostate cancer, and animal evidence.
- **Patient relevance:** Mechanistic context for dairy concerns, especially the IGF-I pathway.
- **Main finding:** The review found moderate evidence that milk and dairy protein intake increase circulating IGF-I and IGFBP-3 modestly, and moderate evidence that prostate cancer risk increases with IGF-I.
- **Limitations:** Mostly risk and biomarker biology, not direct proof that whole-milk avoidance after diagnosis improves recurrence or survival.
- **Questions for clinician:** Does IGF biology support a general plant-forward/metabolic-health approach while avoiding claims that one food change controls cancer?

#### Circulating IGF-I and prostate cancer risk: individual participant meta-analysis

- **Citation:** Travis RC, Appleby PN, Martin RM, et al. *Cancer Research*. 2016.
- **DOI/PMID:** DOI [10.1158/0008-5472.CAN-15-1551](https://doi.org/10.1158/0008-5472.CAN-15-1551); PMID [26921328](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26921328/)
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26921328/
- **Date:** 2016 Apr 15
- **Category:** Nutrition; Hormones; Risk Stratification
- **Evidence type:** Individual participant data meta-analysis.
- **Population:** Up to 10,554 prostate cancer cases and 13,618 controls from 17 prospective and two cross-sectional studies.
- **Patient relevance:** Shows IGF-I is relevant to prostate cancer biology, independent of any one diet theory.
- **Main finding:** In prospective studies, higher IGF-I was associated with higher prostate cancer risk; after mutual adjustment, IGF-I remained associated with risk.
- **Limitations:** Incidence/risk evidence, not a post-diagnosis dairy intervention trial.
- **Questions for clinician:** Should IGF-I biology be treated as a reason for overall metabolic-health diet quality rather than an isolated supplement or dairy rule?

#### Plant-Based Diets and Disease Progression in Men With Prostate Cancer

- **Patient relevance:** Modern postdiagnosis evidence on plant-based dietary patterns and progression.

#### Effect of a behavioral intervention to increase vegetable consumption: MEAL randomized clinical trial

- **Citation:** Parsons JK, et al. *JAMA*. 2020.

- **Category:** Nutrition; Active Surveillance
- **Evidence type:** Randomized clinical trial.

- **Patient relevance:** High-quality test of a vegetable-rich diet intervention in early-stage prostate cancer.

### Fasting mimicking diet, protein, IGF-1, and mTOR

#### Fasting mimicking diet and markers/risk factors for aging, diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular disease

- **Citation:** Wei M, Brandhorst S, Shelehchi M, et al. *Science Translational Medicine*. 2017.
- **DOI/PMID:** DOI [10.1126/scitranslmed.aai8700](https://doi.org/10.1126/scitranslmed.aai8700); PMID [28202779](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28202779/)
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28202779/
- **Date:** 2017 Feb 15
- **Category:** Nutrition; Metabolic Health; Adjacent evidence
- **Evidence type:** Randomized trial in generally healthy adults, not prostate-cancer patients.
- **Population:** 100 generally healthy US participants randomized to unrestricted diet versus monthly 5-day FMD cycles, with crossover.
- **Patient relevance:** Provides the human biomarker basis for Longo's FMD framework: a low-calorie, low-sugar, low-protein, higher-unsaturated-fat periodic diet designed to reduce risk markers without continuous calorie restriction.
- **Main finding:** Three monthly FMD cycles reduced body weight, body fat, blood pressure, fasting glucose, IGF-1, triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, and CRP, with no serious adverse events reported in this selected population.
- **Limitations:** Not a prostate-cancer study; biomarker/risk-factor endpoints, not cancer progression or survival; participants were selected and monitored.
- **Questions for clinician:** If FMD is considered, what is the goal: weight/metabolic markers, treatment tolerance, biomarker change, or a cancer endpoint — and is there evidence for that endpoint in my disease state?

#### The impact of a fasting mimicking diet on metabolic health in prostate cancer: pilot implementation study

- **Citation:** Fay-Watt V, O'Connor S, Roshan D, Romeo AC, Longo VD, Sullivan FJ. *Prostate Cancer and Prostatic Diseases*. 2023.
- **DOI/PMID:** DOI [10.1038/s41391-022-00528-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41391-022-00528-3); PMID [35314788](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35314788/)
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35314788/
- **Date:** 2023
- **Category:** Nutrition; Metabolic Health; ADT/supportive care
- **Evidence type:** Prospective pilot implementation study.
- **Population:** 35 prostate-cancer patients with metabolic features; 29 completed 3-monthly cycles of a 4-consecutive-day packaged FMD.
- **Patient relevance:** Direct prostate-cancer human evidence for periodic FMD as a metabolic-health intervention.
- **Main finding:** Reported improvements included weight, abdominal circumference, blood pressure, glucose, cholesterol, and PSA-related measures after FMD cycles.
- **Limitations:** Small, non-randomized, short follow-up, metabolic/biomarker focus, and not proof that FMD slows progression or improves survival.
- **Questions for clinician:** Would any fasting-like approach be safe given current treatment, weight trend, muscle mass, diabetes medications, kidney function, blood pressure medicines, frailty, and treatment goals?

#### Fasting mimicking diet as an adjunct to neoadjuvant chemotherapy for breast cancer: DIRECT trial

- **Citation:** de Groot S, Lugtenberg RT, Cohen D, et al. *Nature Communications*. 2020.
- **DOI/PMID:** DOI [10.1038/s41467-020-16138-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-16138-3); PMID [32576828](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32576828/)

#### Low protein intake is associated with a major reduction in IGF-1, cancer, and overall mortality in the 65 and younger but not older population

- **Citation:** Levine ME, Suarez JA, Brandhorst S, et al. *Cell Metabolism*. 2014.

## Clinician discussion checklist

- Which phase am I in: screening/risk reduction, active surveillance, surgery/radiation, ADT/systemic therapy, or survivorship?
- Do I need referral to an oncology dietitian, especially if losing weight, gaining weight on ADT, diabetic, frail, or using supplements?
- Are there foods/supplements that could interact with anticoagulants, surgery, radiation side effects, ADT, chemotherapy, kidney disease, or diabetes medications?
- Should the focus be Mediterranean-style, plant-forward, high-fiber diet quality rather than single supplements?
- How should calcium and dairy decisions balance whole-milk recurrence signals, low-fat/non-fat dairy uncertainty, non-dairy calcium sources, and bone health, especially if on ADT?
- If I avoid whole/full-cream milk, what exact calcium/protein replacement should I use, and should vitamin D, DEXA, kidney-stone risk, cardiovascular risk, or ADT bone protection change the plan?
- If considering fish oil or omega-3 supplements, what are the bleeding, medication, and cardiovascular considerations?
- If considering FMD, fasting, or calorie restriction, am I an appropriate candidate given treatment phase, BMI, recent weight loss, frailty, diabetes medications, kidney disease, blood pressure, and muscle mass?
- Does protein advice need to differ by age and goal: lower animal-protein/IGF-1/mTOR exposure in midlife versus adequate protein plus resistance training to protect muscle during ADT or older age?
- Should I specifically minimize processed meat, whole milk, poultry with skin, and high whole-egg intake while using fish, legumes, soy foods, nuts/seeds, skinless poultry, or low-fat/non-fat dairy if appropriate?
- What outcomes should be tracked: weight, waist, muscle/strength, DEXA/body composition, lipids, glucose/HbA1c, blood pressure, bowel symptoms, fatigue, and treatment tolerance?

## Repository interpretation

This evidence strengthens the Nutrition category by adding a structured distinction between:

1. **Incidence prevention claims** — diet pattern evidence is suggestive but not definitive; avoid single-food/supplement certainty.
2. **Post-diagnosis associations** — plant-forward, Mediterranean, and low-inflammatory patterns look promising but are mostly observational.
3. **Active surveillance trials** — increased vegetables alone did not reduce progression in MEAL; omega-3/low omega-6 diet changed a biomarker in CAPFISH-3 but needs longer-term clinical outcomes.
4. **Longo/FMD/protein-IGF-mTOR framework** — valuable as an adjunct metabolic-health and research hypothesis, especially for supervised short periodic FMD cycles and plant-forward protein thinking, but prostate-cancer clinical outcome proof is limited and safety depends heavily on age, ADT status, muscle mass, diabetes/kidney disease, frailty, and treatment phase.
5. **Treatment/survivorship support** — nutrition is strongest as cardiometabolic, weight, muscle, bowel, bone-health, and quality-of-life support, especially during ADT and survivorship. The UCSF video adds useful specificity: processed meat, saturated fat, whole milk, poultry with skin, and high egg intake deserve more caution than low-fat/non-fat dairy or modest egg intake.
