## Accepted items

### 1) Real-world long-term urinary, bowel, sexual, and clinician-graded toxicity after prostate radiotherapy

- **Citation:** Heumann P, Romero JCR, Aguado Barrera ME, Aguado Flor E, Avuzzi B, Azria D, et al. **Early and late CTCAE and patient-reported outcomes following prostate radiotherapy in the REQUITE cohort.** *Clinical and Translational Radiation Oncology.* Published online 2026-07-02; September 2026 issue. DOI: [10.1016/j.ctro.2026.101219](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctro.2026.101219). PMID: [42500362](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42500362/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Prospective, multinational cohort with longitudinal clinician-reported CTCAE and patient-reported outcomes; 1,760 men with non-metastatic prostate cancer treated with curative-intent external-beam radiotherapy and/or brachytherapy, with follow-up up to 8 years.
- **Category/categories:** Side Effects & Quality of Life; Localized Treatment; Shared Decision-Making.
- **Selection score:** 8.4/10.
- **Summary:** This study adds long-term real-world adverse-event data after prostate radiotherapy, stratified by treatment modality and baseline patient factors. Clinician-reported grade ≥2 long-term gastrointestinal/genitourinary symptoms were most common for proctitis and haematuria after EBRT-only treatment; post-prostatectomy radiotherapy patients had notable urinary-incontinence burden. Grade 3 events were uncommon except late urinary incontinence in post-prostatectomy patients.
- **Main finding:** The cohort supports discussing radiotherapy toxicity as a spectrum that depends on baseline symptoms, whether radiotherapy is definitive or post-prostatectomy, and which endpoints are clinician-rated versus patient-reported.
- **Patient relevance:** A patient comparing surgery, radiation, combined therapy, or salvage/post-prostatectomy radiation can ask for individualized estimates of urinary leakage, haematuria, bowel/proctitis symptoms, sexual dysfunction, fatigue, and the local team's mitigation and follow-up plan.
- **Limitations:** Observational cohort rather than randomized treatment comparison; treatments were delivered in 2014–2016 and may not fully represent the newest planning, image guidance, dose constraints, spacer use, or hypofractionation approaches. Outcome completeness and reporting differ by endpoint.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Radiotherapy decisions should be reviewed with radiation oncology and urology in the context of risk group, prior prostatectomy status, baseline urinary/bowel/sexual function, anticoagulation, inflammatory bowel disease, comorbidities, and patient priorities.
- **Changes to decision map/category page:** **Yes.** Filed under Side Effects & Quality of Life as a high-relevance toxicity/outcome anchor; it does not create a simple ranking of treatment options.

### 2) Treatments for ADT-related hot flashes in men with prostate cancer

- **Citation:** Schaldemose EL, Poulsen MH, Nørby B, Madsen CV. **Treatment of Hot Flashes in Men With Prostate Cancer Undergoing Androgen Deprivation Therapy.** *The Prostate.* Published 2026-08-02. DOI: [10.1002/pros.70229](https://doi.org/10.1002/pros.70229). PMID: [42542970](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42542970/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Systematic review of pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic interventions for hot flashes caused by castration therapy/ADT in men with prostate cancer; 35 included studies.
- **Category/categories:** Side Effects & Quality of Life; Advanced & Recurrent Disease; Localized Treatment; Therapies.
- **Selection score:** 8.2/10.
- **Summary:** The review evaluated hormonal agents, SSRIs, gabapentin, oxybutynin, clonidine, acupuncture, cognitive behavioral therapy, and supplements. Across blinded pharmacologic studies, hot-flash frequency reductions varied widely and placebo responses were substantial. Hormonal agents appeared more effective but may be problematic in prostate-cancer contexts; nonhormonal options may be safer for some men but can have smaller or uncertain benefits.
- **Main finding:** ADT hot flashes are common and potentially debilitating, and there are multiple clinician-discussion options, but treatment choice requires balancing symptom relief against cancer context, cardiovascular/thrombotic risk, drug interactions, cognition/falls, and other side effects.
- **Patient relevance:** A patient on ADT can ask whether symptoms are severe enough to treat, which nonhormonal options fit their medication list and comorbidities, whether behavioral approaches are available, and how response and adverse effects would be monitored.
- **Limitations:** The evidence base is heterogeneous, often small, and not all interventions have robust prostate-cancer-specific randomized data. Supplement evidence should be treated cautiously.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Do not self-start hormonal agents, antidepressants, gabapentin, oxybutynin, clonidine, or supplements without clinician/pharmacist review. Potential harms include sedation, dizziness/falls, anticholinergic effects, blood-pressure effects, mood/sexual effects, QT or interaction concerns, and possible conflict with cancer-treatment goals.
- **Changes to decision map/category page:** **Yes.** Filed under Side Effects & Quality of Life as ADT supportive-care context; it supports questions, not self-treatment.

### 3) PSMA PET era management of de novo miN1 prostate cancer

- **Citation:** McMaster T, Louey CYM, Santucci J, Hobson C, Chen DD, Sathianathen N, et al. **Management of De Novo miN1 Prostate Cancer in the PSMA-PET Era.** *The Prostate.* Published 2026-08-02. DOI: [10.1002/pros.70231](https://doi.org/10.1002/pros.70231). PMID: [42542964](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42542964/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Narrative/comprehensive review of PSMA PET-detected regional nodal disease (N1M0/miN1) at initial staging.
- **Category/categories:** Imaging & Biomarkers; Risk Stratification & Staging; Advanced & Recurrent Disease; Localized Treatment.
- **Selection score:** 7.9/10.
- **Summary:** PSMA PET identifies nodal disease that conventional imaging may miss, creating a modern group of patients with image-only regional nodal metastases. The review emphasizes improved staging accuracy and frequent management changes, but also notes imperfect sensitivity for microscopic disease and limited prospective evidence that PSMA-driven management improves long-term survival.
- **Main finding:** PSMA PET can change staging and treatment fields/intensity, but patients should understand the uncertainty created by stage migration and the difference between detecting more disease and proving better outcomes.
- **Patient relevance:** A patient newly staged as N1M0/miN1 on PSMA PET can ask whether management is being based on conventional-risk evidence, PSMA-era evidence, or clinical-trial data; whether treatment is local, nodal-field, systemic, or combined; and what uncertainty remains.
- **Limitations:** Review article rather than new randomized outcome data; many treatment paradigms are extrapolated from conventional imaging or retrospective PSMA-era cohorts.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** PSMA PET findings should be interpreted by experienced nuclear medicine/radiology and multidisciplinary prostate-cancer teams. Small nodes/micrometastases, false positives, and stage migration can affect treatment intensity and side-effect burden.
- **Changes to decision map/category page:** **Yes.** Filed under Imaging & Biomarkers and Advanced/Recurrent Disease as PSMA-era staging context.

### 4) Standardizing serial prostate MRI in active surveillance with PRECISE scoring

- **Citation:** Firoozeh N, Ahn H, Baroni R, Muglia VF, Purysko A, Schieda N, et al. **Standardizing serial prostate MRI in active surveillance: a practical review of PRECISE scoring.** *Abdominal Radiology.* Published 2026-08-01. DOI: [10.1007/s00261-026-05702-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00261-026-05702-y). PMID: [42541554](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42541554/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Practical review of PRECISE/PRECISE v2 scoring for serial MRI monitoring in men on active surveillance.
- **Category/categories:** Active Surveillance; Imaging & Biomarkers; Diagnostic Workup; Risk Stratification & Staging.
- **Selection score:** 7.7/10.
- **Summary:** The review explains how PRECISE scoring complements PI-RADS by describing change over time rather than baseline lesion detection. It highlights PRECISE as most useful as a structured rule-out/stability framework, while MRI progression has more limited positive predictive value for pathology upgrading.
- **Main finding:** Serial MRI can support active-surveillance monitoring, but it should not be treated as a standalone replacement for clinical review, PSA kinetics/density, biopsy decisions, or pathology.
- **Patient relevance:** A patient on active surveillance can ask whether follow-up MRIs are being reported with PRECISE or another structured comparison method, whether image quality is adequate, and what MRI change would trigger repeat biopsy or treatment discussion.
- **Limitations:** Review article; PRECISE performance depends on MRI quality, reader expertise, consistent acquisition, lesion visibility, and integration with histology/PSA.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Stable MRI does not guarantee absence of upgrading, and MRI progression does not automatically mean treatment is required. Decisions should be made with urology/radiology input and a defined surveillance protocol.
- **Changes to decision map/category page:** **Yes.** Filed under Active Surveillance and Imaging & Biomarkers as monitoring-process context.

### 5) Long-term outcomes stratified by 4Kscore after prostate-cancer evaluation

- **Citation:** Nativ O, Guerard T, Williams A, Cortizas E, Szczotka Z, Cuvert M, et al. **Long-term outcomes of men stratified by 4Kscore: a retrospective analysis.** *Urologic Oncology.* Published 2026-08-01. DOI: [10.1016/j.urolonc.2026.06.025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.urolonc.2026.06.025). PMID: [42542399](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42542399/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Retrospective long-term follow-up study; 113 men who had 4Kscore testing before prostate-cancer evaluation, most with baseline MRI, median follow-up 7.3 years.
- **Category/categories:** Screening & Early Detection; Diagnostic Workup; Imaging & Biomarkers; Risk Stratification & Staging.
- **Selection score:** 7.5/10.
- **Summary:** Men were grouped by low, intermediate, and high 4Kscore. Clinically significant prostate cancer occurred more often in higher-score groups; no high-grade cancers, metastases, or prostate-cancer-specific deaths occurred in the low-risk group in this small cohort.
- **Main finding:** The study supports the idea that 4Kscore can add durable risk information to biopsy/MRI discussions, but it is not large enough to be a definitive rule for avoiding diagnosis or follow-up.
- **Patient relevance:** A patient with an elevated PSA can ask whether reflex biomarkers such as 4Kscore are appropriate in their setting, how the result would interact with MRI, PSA density, family/genetic risk, prior biopsy, infection/inflammation context, and whether it would actually change a biopsy decision.
- **Limitations:** Small retrospective cohort, likely selection bias, wide uncertainty, and event numbers too low for some outcomes. Results may not generalize across ancestry groups, PSA ranges, MRI pathways, or health systems.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** A low biomarker score should not be treated as ruling out cancer. Persistent PSA concern, abnormal MRI/DRE, high-risk family/genetic features, or symptoms still need clinician-directed evaluation.
- **Changes to decision map/category page:** **Yes.** Filed under Screening & Early Detection / Imaging & Biomarkers as biomarker triage context.

### 6) Pre-consultation education tool for intermediate-risk prostate cancer

- **Citation:** Borras-Osorio M, Kowalchuk RO, Preston HR, Rajkumar AW, Uptmor AM, Davis BJ, et al. **Real-world implementation of a pre-consultation patient educational tool for intermediate risk prostate cancer.** *Clinical and Translational Radiation Oncology.* Published online 2026-07-10; September 2026 issue. DOI: [10.1016/j.ctro.2026.101224](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctro.2026.101224). PMID: [42519504](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42519504/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Real-world implementation/usability study; 395 newly diagnosed intermediate-risk prostate-cancer patients received a plain-language educational tool before consultation, with 79.7% questionnaire completion.
- **Category/categories:** Shared Decision-Making; Localized Treatment; Patient Education; Side Effects & Quality of Life.
- **Selection score:** 7.6/10.
- **Summary:** Most respondents reported improved preparedness for discussion and better understanding of diagnosis after using the tool. Treatment-selection differences before versus after implementation were exploratory and should not be interpreted as proving the tool causes better choices.
- **Main finding:** Plain-language pre-consultation materials may improve readiness for shared decision-making in intermediate-risk disease, especially when treatment choices are complex and consultation time is limited.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients can ask clinics for balanced written/visual decision aids before appointments and use them to prepare questions about active surveillance eligibility, surgery, radiation options, focal therapy uncertainty, ADT use, side effects, logistics, and follow-up.
- **Limitations:** Single implementation setting, self-reported preparedness, possible response bias, and non-randomized comparison of treatment selections. It does not prove improved long-term outcomes or decisional quality.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Educational tools should supplement—not replace—individualized consultation with urology, radiation oncology, medical oncology when relevant, and specialist nursing/allied-health support.
- **Changes to decision map/category page:** **Yes.** Filed under Shared Decision-Making as practical patient-preparation evidence.

## Overall interpretation for patients

This week’s most patient-relevant theme is **how evidence is translated into practical questions before and after treatment**. Recent studies reinforce the need to ask about long-term radiotherapy side effects, ADT hot-flash management, PSMA PET stage migration, structured MRI monitoring during active surveillance, biomarker triage limits, and better pre-consultation education. None of these items should be read as a stand-alone instruction to choose or avoid a treatment; they are prompts for clinician discussion based on a patient’s risk group, imaging/pathology, baseline function, comorbidities, preferences, and local expertise.
