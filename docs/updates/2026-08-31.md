## Accepted items

### 1) Cardio-oncology referral improved risk-factor control in men with prostate cancer, but event reduction remains uncertain

- **Citation:** Leong DP, Higano C, Cano Garcia C, Fradet V, Siemens DR, Niazi T, Shayegan B, Guha A, et al. **Specialist Referral for Cardiovascular Risk in Patients With Prostate Cancer: A Randomized Clinical Trial.** *JAMA Internal Medicine.* Published 2026-08-30. DOI: [10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.4773](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.4773). PMID: [42669035](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42669035/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Pragmatic randomized clinical trial; 2,487 patients with prostate cancer at 55 sites in eight countries, including men recently diagnosed, receiving first ADT within 6 months, or about to start ADT.
- **Category/categories:** Side effects & quality of life; cardiovascular survivorship; ADT supportive care; advanced/recurrent disease; shared decision-making.
- **Rubric score:** 9.6/10.
- **Summary:** Usual care plus routine internist/cardiologist referral improved a hierarchical cardiovascular/risk-factor composite compared with usual care. The signal was driven mostly by better cholesterol control from protocol-mandated statin use; there was no clear reduction in cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, or heart failure.
- **Main finding:** Cardiovascular risk management is a real treatment-pathway issue in prostate cancer, especially around ADT, but this trial does not prove that routine referral prevents hard cardiovascular events.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients starting or already receiving ADT can ask whether they need baseline cardiovascular-risk review, blood pressure and cholesterol optimization, diabetes/smoking/exercise support, medication reconciliation, and whether cardio-oncology referral is appropriate for their baseline risk.
- **Limitations:** The intervention included statin use irrespective of usual guideline indications, so applicability depends on local cardiovascular-prevention guidance and individual risk. The hard-event endpoint was not clearly improved.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Do not start a statin, blood-pressure medicine, aspirin, supplement, or exercise program solely from this study. Cardiovascular prevention has contraindications, interaction issues, and monitoring needs that should be reviewed with a GP, cardiologist/internist, oncologist, or pharmacist.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to side-effects/QoL and advanced/recurrent disease as an ADT/cardio-oncology discussion prompt.

### 2) Companion analysis: who may benefit most from routine cardiovascular specialist referral remains a subgroup question

- **Citation:** Cano Garcia C, Pinthus J, Avezum A, Devereaux PJ, Klotz L, Higano C, Fradet V, Selvanayagam J, et al. **Identifying Patients With Prostate Cancer Who Benefit Most From Routine Cardiovascular Specialist Referral.** *JACC: CardioOncology.* Published 2026-08-30. DOI: [10.1016/j.jaccao.2026.08.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaccao.2026.08.001). PMID: [42669076](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42669076/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Prespecified subgroup analysis of the RADICAL PC-2 randomized trial population.
- **Category/categories:** Side effects & quality of life; cardiovascular survivorship; ADT supportive care; shared decision-making.
- **Rubric score:** 8.3/10.
- **Summary:** This companion analysis explored whether baseline cardiovascular risk identified patients more likely to benefit from specialist referral. It should be read as a refinement of the main RADICAL PC-2 trial rather than as a separate practice-changing result.
- **Main finding:** Baseline cardiovascular risk may help triage who most needs specialist optimization, but subgroup findings should not override individualized clinical judgment.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients can ask, “What is my baseline cardiovascular risk before ADT or systemic therapy, and does that risk justify referral to a cardiologist, internist, cardio-oncology clinic, exercise physiologist, or dietitian?”
- **Limitations:** Subgroup analyses can be underpowered and hypothesis-generating even when prespecified.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Higher cardiovascular risk should prompt clinician review, not self-directed medication changes.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Folded into the cardio-oncology note on side-effects/QoL and shared decision-making.

### 3) Urine proteomic biomarkers may complement PSA, risk calculators, and MRI before first biopsy

- **Citation:** Morillo AC, Jobre KN, Lendinez Cano G, Blanca-Pedregosa A, Lopez Ruiz D, Parada J, Heidegger I, Culig Z, et al. **Prospective validation of a urine-based proteomics test for predicting clinically significant prostate cancer in biopsy-naïve patients.** *British Journal of Cancer.* Published 2026-08-29. DOI: [10.1038/s41416-026-03602-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41416-026-03602-y). PMID: [42668309](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42668309/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Prospective validation study; 161 biopsy-naïve patients at risk for clinically significant prostate cancer who underwent pre-biopsy urine capillary electrophoresis-mass spectrometry, mpMRI, and biopsy; 100 radical-prostatectomy patients were also evaluated for pathology correlation.
- **Category/categories:** Screening/early detection; diagnostic workup; MRI-first pathways; biomarkers/genomics; biopsy decision-making.
- **Rubric score:** 9.0/10.
- **Summary:** A 19-biomarker urine model achieved AUC 0.79 for clinically significant prostate cancer, outperforming PSA, ERSPC risk calculation, PSA density, and mpMRI in this dataset. A nomogram combining the urine model with mpMRI achieved AUC 0.82, with high specificity but only moderate sensitivity.
- **Main finding:** Urine proteomics may become a useful add-on to PSA/risk-calculator/MRI pathways, but it is not a stand-alone rule-out test.
- **Patient relevance:** A patient facing first biopsy can ask whether biomarkers are available, externally validated, reimbursed, and likely to change the biopsy decision in their specific PSA, PSA-density, PI-RADS, DRE, family-history/genetic-risk, and prostate-volume context.
- **Limitations:** Modest sample size, specialist-center setting, commercial/employment conflicts disclosed for some authors, and the sensitivity of combined approaches means missed clinically significant cancers remain possible.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Biomarker reassurance should not be used alone to avoid biopsy or follow-up where MRI, PSA density, DRE, symptoms, family history, ancestry, or genetic risk remains concerning.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to diagnostic workup and imaging/biomarkers as a promising but validation-dependent pre-biopsy triage tool.

### 4) Sexual side-effect discussions may be one-way even when clinicians ask about sexual activity

- **Citation:** Stewart SJ, Roberts L, Brindle LA. **“Are you sexually active?”: Unilateral informing patterns in communicating the sexual impact of prostate cancer treatment.** *Social Science & Medicine.* Published 2026 Sep. DOI: [10.1016/j.socscimed.2026.119528](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2026.119528). PMID: [42665307](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42665307/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Conversation-analysis study of diagnostic and treatment consultations involving people with low- or intermediate-risk prostate cancer and couples where sexual impact of treatment was discussed.
- **Category/categories:** Side effects & quality of life; sexual function; localized treatment; shared decision-making; clinician communication.
- **Rubric score:** 8.5/10.
- **Summary:** The study found that the question “Are you sexually active?” often acted as a springboard for clinicians to deliver information about sexual effects, without necessarily exploring the couple's response, priorities, relationship context, or rehabilitation needs.
- **Main finding:** Sexual-function counseling is not just whether side effects are mentioned; the consultation needs space for patient and partner goals, baseline function, distress, and recovery planning.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients and partners can prepare to ask about expected erectile, orgasmic, libido, climacturia, penile-length, fertility, intimacy, and relationship effects; available rehabilitation; timing; costs; and who provides follow-up support.
- **Limitations:** Conversation-analysis studies are context-rich but may not generalize across countries, clinics, risk groups, languages, cultures, or consultation formats.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Sexual rehabilitation medicines/devices and testosterone-related questions can have contraindications and should be discussed with clinicians familiar with the cancer risk group and cardiovascular status.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to side-effects/QoL and shared decision-making as a communication-quality issue.

### 5) 177Lu-PSMA-617 treatment burden: median healthcare-contact days were measurable but relatively low in one mCRPC cohort

- **Citation:** Scharf Z, Muniz M, Tchitchkan E, Rami A, Ravi PK, Orme JJ, Tarhini A, Jacene HA, et al. **An Assessment of Time Toxicity in Patients Receiving [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 for Metastatic Castration-Resistant Prostate Cancer (mCRPC).** *The Oncologist.* Published 2026-08-17. DOI: [10.1093/oncolo/oyag327](https://doi.org/10.1093/oncolo/oyag327). PMID: [42606561](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42606561/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Multi-institutional retrospective cohort; 135 heavily pretreated mCRPC patients initiating [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 between April 2022 and March 2023.
- **Category/categories:** Advanced/recurrent disease; PSMA theranostics; treatment burden; side effects & quality of life; shared decision-making.
- **Rubric score:** 8.5/10.
- **Summary:** Patients completed a median of five cycles over 7.6 months. Median healthcare contact was 20 days, about 9.5% of at-risk days; 11% experienced high time toxicity. Lower baseline hemoglobin was independently associated with greater time toxicity.
- **Main finding:** Treatment burden is quantifiable and should be discussed alongside survival, PSA/radiographic response, dry mouth, marrow/kidney reserve, fatigue, travel, caregiver time, and access delays.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients considering radioligand therapy can ask how many visits, scans, blood tests, radiation-safety steps, travel days, possible admissions, and caregiver supports are realistically expected locally.
- **Limitations:** Retrospective, US-centered/multi-institutional cohort; treatment logistics vary by country, center, reimbursement, distance, radiopharmaceutical supply, baseline anemia, and disease pace.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** This should not be read as saying treatment is easy or suitable for everyone. Marrow function, kidney function, salivary-gland effects, symptoms, prior therapies, and goals of care require specialist review.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to advanced/recurrent disease and side-effects/QoL as a treatment-burden measure.

### 6) Non-pharmacological fatigue management: exercise, education, sleep, psychosocial, and multidisciplinary supports remain clinician-discussion items

- **Citation:** Jiang M, Xu M, Chen Q, Fu X, Lin X, Chen X. **Best evidence summary for non-pharmacological management of cancer-related fatigue in patients with prostate cancer.** *Geriatric Nursing.* Published 2026-08-20. DOI: [10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104285](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gerinurse.2026.104285). PMID: [42623906](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42623906/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Best-evidence summary drawing on evidence-based databases, guideline networks, professional associations, guidelines, clinical decisions, expert consensuses, and systematic reviews through November 2024.
- **Category/categories:** Side effects & quality of life; fatigue; survivorship; ADT supportive care; exercise; nursing/allied-health care pathways.
- **Rubric score:** 8.0/10.
- **Summary:** The review synthesized 23 pieces of evidence across nine domains for non-pharmacological management of prostate-cancer-related fatigue. The practical message is that fatigue should be assessed and managed through structured, multimodal supports rather than dismissed as inevitable.
- **Main finding:** Fatigue conversations should include assessment of reversible causes and tailored non-drug strategies such as supervised exercise/physical activity, education, sleep support, psychosocial care, energy conservation, nutrition review, and multidisciplinary referral where appropriate.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients can ask whether fatigue is due to ADT, radiation, chemotherapy, anemia, sleep disturbance, depression/anxiety, pain, deconditioning, infection, other medicines, or other conditions, and which supports are safe for their bone/metastasis/cardiovascular status.
- **Limitations:** A best-evidence summary is not a new randomized trial; the included evidence types and quality vary, and recommendations need tailoring to metastatic/bone disease, frailty, comorbidities, treatment phase, and local service availability.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Exercise and fatigue plans should be individualized, especially with bone metastases, anemia, falls risk, cardiac disease, severe symptoms, or treatment complications. New or severe fatigue can require medical assessment.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to side-effects/QoL as a survivorship/supportive-care prompt.

## Overall patient-facing takeaway

This week's strongest themes are **cardiovascular risk management around prostate-cancer care/ADT**, **validation-dependent urine biomarkers before first biopsy**, **better sexual-side-effect communication**, **making radioligand therapy time burden visible**, and **structured non-drug fatigue support**. None of these findings is a stand-alone instruction to change screening, imaging, biopsy, medication, surgery, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, or survivorship care. They are best used to prepare specific questions for a GP, urologist, radiation oncologist, medical oncologist, nuclear-medicine physician, cardiologist/cardio-oncology clinic, pharmacist, pelvic-floor/sexual-health clinician, exercise physiologist, psychologist, specialist nurse, or other qualified clinician depending on context.
