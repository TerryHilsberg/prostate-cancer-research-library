### Patient-facing bottom line

Daily low-dose aspirin is biologically plausible as an anti-metastasis adjunct, especially through platelet and T-cell mechanisms, but prostate-cancer-specific clinical evidence is mixed and largely observational. It should be treated as a clinician-discussion item, not a proven prostate-cancer anti-metastasis treatment.

### Key clinician questions

- Do I have an independent cardiovascular indication for low-dose aspirin?
- What is my personal bleeding risk, including age, ulcer history, kidney function, blood pressure, anticoagulants/antiplatelets, NSAIDs, supplements, procedures, surgery, biopsy, or radiotherapy?
- Is my prostate cancer high-risk enough that the observational aspirin signals are worth discussing?
- Would aspirin complicate biopsy, surgery, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, clinical-trial eligibility, or management of bleeding/urinary side effects?

## Accepted items

### 1) Organized PSA screening: ERSPC nonattendance analysis after 20 years

- **Citation:** Leenen RCA, Mulder EFH, Remmers S, de Vos II, Talala K, Hugosson J, Arnsrud-Godtman R, Hond ED, et al. **Prostate Cancer Mortality in Men not Attending a Population-based Screening Program: The 'Good', the 'Bad', and the 'Ugly' After 20-yr Follow-up in the European Randomised study of Screening for Prostate Cancer.** *European Urology Open Science.* Published online 2026-07-30; September 2026 issue. DOI: [10.1016/j.euros.2026.06.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euros.2026.06.009). PMID: [42571431](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42571431/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Secondary analysis of the randomized ERSPC screening trial; 161,380 men aged 55–69 years across seven ERSPC centres, with up to 20 years of follow-up.
- **Category/categories:** Screening & early detection; PSA interpretation; guidelines/care pathways.
- **Rubric score:** 8.6/10.
- **Summary:** Within the invited screening arm, 17% were “never-attenders.” At 20 years, prostate-cancer-specific mortality was 1.4% in never-attenders, 1.2% in the control arm, and 1.0% in men who attended at least one screening round.
- **Main finding:** Men invited to screening who never attended had higher observed prostate-cancer mortality than the non-invited control group, while attendees had lower observed mortality than controls. This strengthens the public-health point that participation patterns and baseline health/risk differences matter when interpreting screening-program results.
- **Patient relevance:** For a potential patient, the study does **not** mean PSA screening is automatically right for every man. It does support discussing screening as an organized, quality-controlled pathway—risk assessment, PSA thresholds, MRI/biopsy triage, and follow-up—rather than one-off opportunistic testing.
- **Limitations:** Attendance was not randomized; never-attenders may differ from attenders in health status, access, socioeconomic factors, risk profile, or health-seeking behaviour. The analysis lacks detailed determinants of attendance.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** PSA screening can reduce advanced presentation for some men but can also cause false positives, overdiagnosis, anxiety, biopsy harms, and treatment side effects. Age, life expectancy, family/genetic risk, symptoms, prior PSA values, infections/inflammation, medications, and personal preferences should be reviewed with a clinician.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added a screening-category note emphasizing organized screening pathways and participation bias.

### 2) Czech nationwide screening pilot: early feasibility of PSA-to-MRI pathway

- **Citation:** Koudelková M, Hejcmanová K, Babjuk M, Zachoval R, Ferda J, Bratt O, Chloupková R, Ngo O, et al. **Implementation and First Results of the Czech Nationwide Prostate Cancer Screening Pilot Program.** *European Urology Open Science.* Published online 2026-07-25; September 2026 issue. DOI: [10.1016/j.euros.2026.07.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euros.2026.07.003). PMID: [42564931](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42564931/)
- **Evidence type and population:** National screening-program implementation report; first 12 months of a publicly reimbursed Czech pilot targeting men aged 50–69 years without prior prostate cancer.
- **Category/categories:** Screening & early detection; MRI-first pathways; biopsy triage; guidelines/care pathways.
- **Rubric score:** 8.1/10.
- **Summary:** Screening was offered to 150,498 men; PSA results were available for 146,109 (97.1%). Overall, 8.8% had PSA ≥3.0 µg/L; 67.2% of these men had urological evaluation within 6 months. From 2023 to 2024, the 2-year PSA testing rate rose from 47.4% to 53.3%, and pre-biopsy MRI use increased from 28.0% to 38.3%.
- **Main finding:** A national program can redirect widespread unorganized PSA testing toward a more structured pathway with urologist review and more pre-biopsy MRI use, but cancer-detection, overdiagnosis, biopsy, stage-shift, and mortality outcomes are not yet available.
- **Patient relevance:** This is useful for understanding what “modern screening” increasingly means: PSA is the entry point, but the key patient decision is often what happens next—repeat PSA, risk calculators, MRI, biopsy route, and follow-up.
- **Limitations:** Early implementation data only. Diagnostic outcomes and downstream harms/benefits have not yet been analyzed; applicability outside the Czech health system may vary.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** A PSA threshold is not a diagnosis. Elevated PSA can reflect benign enlargement, inflammation, recent infection, ejaculation, cycling, procedures, or medications. MRI can miss some significant cancers and can also lead to biopsy of lesions that prove indolent.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to screening as a modern organized-screening/MRI-triage example.

### 3) Micro-ultrasound-guided nerve preservation during robotic prostatectomy: interim RCT

- **Citation:** Pedraza AM, Fatterpekar M, Joshi H, Choudhary M, Kacagan C, Mandel A, Arora C, Tillu N, et al. **Saline-assisted Fascial Exposure Microultrasound-guided Nerve Preservation During Robotic Prostatectomy: Interim Analysis of a Randomized Controlled Trial.** *European Urology Oncology.* Published 2026-08-08. DOI: [10.1016/j.euo.2026.07.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euo.2026.07.015). PMID: [42570880](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42570880/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Randomized controlled trial interim analysis; 107 men assigned to SAFE micro-ultrasound-guided robotic prostatectomy or standard robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy.
- **Category/categories:** Localized treatment; surgery; sexual function; side effects/quality of life.
- **Rubric score:** 8.0/10.
- **Summary:** The primary endpoint was erectile-function recovery at 6 months (SHIM ≥17). Recovery was numerically higher with SAFE than standard RARP (52% vs 43%), but the prespecified interim efficacy boundary was not met. Early 6-week recovery appeared higher in exploratory analysis, while continence, complications, and oncologic outcomes were similar between groups.
- **Main finding:** The technique is promising but not practice-changing on this interim analysis; it suggests possible earlier functional recovery without an obvious safety signal, but the primary endpoint was not met.
- **Patient relevance:** Men considering prostatectomy often ask how nerve-sparing and surgeon technique affect erections and continence. This trial supports asking about evidence for a centre’s nerve-sparing approach, baseline erectile function, cancer location, margin risk, and realistic recovery timelines.
- **Limitations:** Interim, small sample, unblinded surgeons, short follow-up, and exploratory early-recovery findings. Longer oncologic and functional follow-up is needed.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Nerve preservation must not compromise cancer control. Suitability depends on tumour location, MRI/biopsy findings, extracapsular-extension risk, baseline function, and surgeon experience. This should not be used to choose surgery or a specific technique without specialist review.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to localized-treatment and side-effect/QoL watchlists as a cautious surgery-function item.

### 4) Radiotherapy side-effect prediction models: systematic review of methodological quality

- **Citation:** Oliveira CC, Jongen CAM, Mikolić A, Incrocci L, Lingsma HF, Roobol MJ, Korfage I, Heemsbergen WD, et al. **Prediction models for adverse events from prostate cancer curative radiotherapy: a systematic review of methodological quality.** *Translational Oncology.* Published 2026-08-08. DOI: [10.1016/j.tranon.2026.102961](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tranon.2026.102961). PMID: [42570550](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42570550/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Systematic review of clinical prediction models for urinary, bowel, sexual, and other adverse events after curative prostate radiotherapy; 35 included studies yielding 107 models.
- **Category/categories:** Side effects & quality of life; localized treatment; radiotherapy decision support.
- **Rubric score:** 8.2/10.
- **Summary:** Of 107 adverse-event prediction models, 22 had external validation, but only two models were classified as low risk of bias using the short-form PROBAST criteria. The authors conclude that ready-to-use models remain scarce and that validation/refinement should be prioritized over creating more unvalidated models.
- **Main finding:** Many models exist, but few are reliable enough for routine individualized counseling. This cautions against overconfident “personal toxicity calculators” unless they have been externally validated and calibrated for a patient’s context.
- **Patient relevance:** When comparing radiation options, patients can ask what side-effect estimates are based on: institutional outcomes, trial data, registry data, dose-volume constraints, baseline urinary/bowel/sexual function, ADT exposure, and validated prediction tools.
- **Limitations:** Review includes studies through August 2024, so very recent models may not be captured. Model performance may differ by radiotherapy technique, dose, image guidance, margins, baseline symptoms, and follow-up definitions.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Prediction tools should support—not replace—clinician counseling. Underestimating urinary, bowel, sexual, fatigue, or ADT-related effects can distort shared decision-making.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to side-effect/QoL and localized-treatment pages as a caution around individualized radiotherapy-toxicity calculators.

### 5) 177Lu-PSMA radioligand therapy in mCRPC: GRADE-assessed meta-analysis

- **Citation:** Mubarika M, Maaz M, Muhammad A, Emmama S, Mazhar Z, Khalid M, Kumari J, Alamin MH, et al. **Efficacy, safety, and quality of life outcomes of [177Lu] Lu-PSMA radioligand therapy in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer: a GRADE-assessed systematic review, meta-analysis and trial sequential analysis.** *International Urology and Nephrology.* Published 2026-08-08. DOI: [10.1007/s11255-026-05329-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11255-026-05329-4). PMID: [42570199](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42570199/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Systematic review/meta-analysis of randomized trials; six RCTs, 2,113 adults with PSMA-positive metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer.
- **Category/categories:** Advanced/recurrent disease; PSMA PET/imaging; radioligand therapy; quality of life.
- **Rubric score:** 8.4/10.
- **Summary:** Intention-to-treat analysis did not show a statistically significant overall-survival improvement (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.62–1.06), but crossover-adjusted sensitivity analysis suggested survival benefit (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.59–0.96). 177Lu-PSMA improved radiographic progression-free survival, PSA response, and delayed quality-of-life deterioration; low-grade dry mouth and grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia were increased.
- **Main finding:** The evidence base for 177Lu-PSMA in PSMA-positive mCRPC continues to strengthen for disease-control and QoL endpoints, while OS interpretation remains affected by crossover, sequencing, and trial heterogeneity.
- **Patient relevance:** For men with advanced PSMA-positive disease, this supports asking when PSMA PET is needed, where 177Lu-PSMA fits relative to taxanes and androgen-receptor pathway inhibitors, and how marrow reserve, dry mouth, kidney function, blood counts, prior treatments, symptoms, and trial eligibility affect suitability.
- **Limitations:** Inter-trial heterogeneity, different control regimens, crossover, PSMA-imaging selection, and evolving treatment sequences. Findings do not automatically apply to hormone-sensitive disease or PSMA-low/negative disease.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Radioligand therapy can involve salivary-gland symptoms, marrow suppression/thrombocytopenia, fatigue, nausea, renal considerations, radiation-safety instructions, and access constraints. It requires nuclear-medicine/oncology review.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to advanced/recurrent disease and side-effect/QoL watchlists; no change to patient-facing sequencing recommendations.

### 6) PSA nadir after intensified therapy in metastatic hormone-sensitive disease

- **Citation:** Di Civita MA, Pappalardo L, De Angelis D, Stellato M, Zacco L, Rota S, Gusmaroli E, Guadalupi V, et al. **Impact of PSA Nadir on Outcomes in Metastatic Hormone-Sensitive Prostate Cancer: A Systematic Review and Reconstructed Individual Patient Data Meta-analysis.** *Targeted Oncology.* Published 2026-08-07. DOI: [10.1007/s11523-026-01234-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11523-026-01234-z). PMID: [42568016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42568016/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Systematic review and reconstructed individual-patient-data meta-analysis; eight studies of metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer treated with androgen-receptor pathway inhibitors or docetaxel, with 1,638 patients for overall-survival analysis.
- **Category/categories:** Advanced/recurrent disease; PSA interpretation; treatment monitoring.
- **Rubric score:** 8.0/10.
- **Summary:** Achieving PSA nadir ≤0.2 ng/mL was associated with longer progression-free survival and overall survival than PSA nadir >0.2 ng/mL in pooled reconstructed analyses.
- **Main finding:** Deep PSA decline remains a strong prognostic marker during first-line intensified therapy for metastatic hormone-sensitive disease, but it is not the same as proof that changing treatment solely to chase a PSA threshold improves outcomes.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients on ADT plus docetaxel or an androgen-receptor pathway inhibitor can ask how PSA response is interpreted alongside symptoms, imaging, testosterone suppression, alkaline phosphatase, clinical risk volume, adverse effects, and treatment goals.
- **Limitations:** Reconstructed IPD from published curves, mixed prospective/retrospective data, variable therapies and follow-up, and prognostic rather than treatment-randomization evidence.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** PSA is important but incomplete. Do not stop, switch, or intensify systemic therapy based only on PSA nadir without oncology review; imaging and clinical status can tell a different story.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to advanced/recurrent disease as a monitoring/prognostic item.

Source: [NICE in-development page](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/indevelopment/gid-ta11534) and [NICE prostate-cancer topic page](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/conditions-and-diseases/cancer/prostate-cancer).

- **Category/categories:** Advanced/recurrent disease; biomarkers/genomics; guidelines/care pathways.
- **Rubric score:** 7.6/10 as a guideline-watchlist item, not final guidance.
- **Summary:** NICE lists an active consultation for talazoparib with enzalutamide in HRR-mutated hormone-sensitive metastatic prostate cancer. This is not final NICE guidance, but it is a relevant watch item because it concerns biomarker-selected treatment intensification.
- **Main finding:** No patient-facing practice change yet; final appraisal wording, eligibility, comparator assumptions, cost-effectiveness, and NHS availability remain to be determined.
- **Patient relevance:** Men with metastatic disease can ask whether germline and/or tumour testing for HRR mutations is appropriate and how results might affect treatment options or clinical-trial eligibility.

## Overall patient-facing takeaway

This week’s strongest themes are **organized screening pathways**, **MRI before biopsy within screening programs**, **cautious interpretation of emerging surgical and radiotherapy decision-support tools**, and **continued refinement of advanced-disease treatment sequencing using PSMA imaging and genomic biomarkers**. None of these items should be read as a stand-alone recommendation to start screening, choose surgery/radiation/systemic therapy, or change treatment. They are best used to prepare specific questions for a urologist, radiation oncologist, medical oncologist, nuclear-medicine physician, genetic counselor, or GP depending on the clinical context.
