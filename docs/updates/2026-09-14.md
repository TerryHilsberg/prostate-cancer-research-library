## Accepted items

### 1) Testosterone monitoring after medium- to long-course ADT plus radiotherapy is a survivorship issue, but testosterone supplementation remains uncertain

- **Citation:** Achard V, Zapatero A, Kishan AU, Blanchard P, Horvath LG, Jereczek-Fossa BA, Tombal B. **Testosterone monitoring and supplementation in patients with intermediate- or high-risk prostate cancer treated with 6–36 months of androgen deprivation therapy and radiotherapy: a systematic literature review.** *Radiotherapy and Oncology.* Published 2026-09-12. DOI: [10.1016/j.radonc.2026.111777](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.radonc.2026.111777). PMID: [42731757](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42731757/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Systematic literature review; 29 studies addressing baseline testosterone, on-treatment testosterone suppression, testosterone recovery after ADT, and limited evidence on testosterone replacement after curative-intent treatment in intermediate- or high-risk localized prostate cancer treated with radiotherapy plus 6–36 months of ADT.
- **Category/categories:** Localized treatment; side effects & quality of life; hormone therapy survivorship; shared decision-making.
- **Rubric score:** 8.8/10.
- **Summary:** The review found that baseline testosterone was not clearly associated with oncologic outcomes, but did predict testosterone recovery after ADT. Deeper on-treatment suppression below the traditional castrate threshold was associated with better outcomes in included studies, while recovery after ADT was highly variable and could take months to years. Evidence for testosterone replacement after curative treatment was described as very limited and observational.
- **Main finding:** Testosterone recovery and persistent hypogonadal symptoms after ADT deserve explicit follow-up, but the evidence does not support self-directed testosterone use or a simple “safe for everyone” conclusion.
- **Patient relevance:** Men starting or finishing ADT with radiotherapy can ask how testosterone will be monitored, what symptoms to report, how long recovery commonly takes after the planned ADT duration, and what non-testosterone supportive options exist for fatigue, sexual symptoms, mood, metabolic changes, bone health, and body composition.
- **Limitations:** The review synthesizes heterogeneous studies rather than a new randomized trial. Testosterone replacement evidence after prostate cancer remains sparse, observational, and highly dependent on risk group, treatment history, PSA stability, time since treatment, and specialist judgment.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Do not start testosterone, “testosterone boosters,” supplements, or hormone manipulation without specialist review. Potential harms include cancer-control uncertainty, PSA interpretation difficulty, erythrocytosis, cardiovascular concerns, fertility/sexual-health issues, drug interactions, and monitoring burden.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to localized treatment and side-effects/QoL as an ADT survivorship monitoring discussion.

### 2) Histology-linked PSMA PET thresholds may support interpretation, but should not replace clinical context or biopsy

- **Citation:** Lindenberg L, Huang E, Ulaner GA, Patel KR, Sonni I, Niknam K, Berenji GR, Peacock S, et al. **Quantitative Thresholds on 18F-DCFPyL PET for Determining Malignancy in Prostate Cancer with Pathologic Correlation.** *Journal of Nuclear Medicine.* Published 2026-09-10. DOI: [10.2967/jnumed.126.272271](https://doi.org/10.2967/jnumed.126.272271). PMID: [42722438](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42722438/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Multicenter retrospective imaging-pathology correlation study; 305 men with prostate cancer and 476 PET regions of interest matched to surgical or biopsy histology after 18F-DCFPyL PSMA PET/CT.
- **Category/categories:** Imaging & biomarkers; PSMA PET; diagnostic workup; staging; biopsy selection.
- **Rubric score:** 8.4/10.
- **Summary:** Multiple PSMA PET quantitative metrics were associated with malignancy. SUVmax-to-blood-pool ratio performed strongly across all lesions, prostate lesions, and metastatic lesions, and combining PET metrics with clinical variables improved discrimination. The authors concluded that quantitative cutoffs may support clinical decision-making, including biopsy selection, but require larger confirmation.
- **Main finding:** PSMA PET interpretation may become more quantitative and reproducible, but thresholds are not a stand-alone cancer diagnosis.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients can ask whether PSMA PET findings are being interpreted qualitatively only or with quantitative parameters, whether the scan type/tracer and local expertise match the evidence, and whether suspicious lesions require MRI, biopsy, pathology correlation, or multidisciplinary review before treatment decisions.
- **Limitations:** Retrospective design, selected patients who had tissue correlation, tracer-specific results, and likely center/expertise effects. Thresholds may not generalize to every scanner, protocol, tracer, lesion size, treatment state, or inflammatory/benign mimic.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** A PSMA-avid lesion can still need confirmation; a low-uptake lesion does not necessarily exclude clinically significant disease. Imaging should be integrated with PSA kinetics, MRI, biopsy, pathology, symptoms, and prior treatment history.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to imaging/biomarkers and diagnostic workup as PSMA PET quantification evidence.

### 3) Plasma infrared molecular fingerprinting shows a partly PSA-independent diagnostic signal, but current discrimination is modest

- **Citation:** Jacob P, Chaloupka M, Eissa T, Rebel J, Aschauer J, Pfundmeier C, Witzens S, Brinkmann I, et al. **Plasma infrared molecular fingerprinting for prostate cancer detection: Prospective evidence for a partially PSA-independent signal.** *European Journal of Cancer.* Published online 2026-07-10; newly indexed/retrieved in this scan window. DOI: [10.1016/j.ejca.2026.116945](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejca.2026.116945). PMID: [42520587](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42520587/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Prospective Lasers4Life diagnostic study; plasma from 2,024 men, including 1,012 treatment-naïve prostate-cancer patients, 752 men with benign prostatic hyperplasia, and 260 asymptomatic controls.
- **Category/categories:** Screening/early detection; diagnostic workup; biomarkers; PSA interpretation.
- **Rubric score:** 8.1/10.
- **Summary:** Infrared molecular fingerprinting distinguished prostate cancer from asymptomatic controls more strongly than from benign prostatic hyperplasia. In an age- and PSA-matched subgroup with PSA below 12 ng/mL, discrimination was statistically better than PSA alone but still modest. The PubMed abstract concludes that the signal is partly independent of PSA, but needs independent multicenter validation, clinically significant-cancer endpoints, and evidence of additive value within established pathways.
- **Main finding:** This is a promising blood-based research biomarker signal, not a ready substitute for PSA, MRI, risk calculators, or biopsy.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients in a PSA gray zone can ask whether any biomarker being offered has independent validation, whether it predicts clinically significant cancer rather than any cancer, and whether it changes the practical decision about repeat PSA, MRI, PSA density, biopsy, or surveillance.
- **Limitations:** Performance was modest in the clinically difficult cancer-versus-BPH comparison, and the study does not establish how to use the test in routine biopsy decisions.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Do not use an experimental or private-market blood biomarker to ignore a concerning PSA trend, abnormal DRE, suspicious MRI, family/genetic risk, symptoms, or clinician advice.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to diagnostic workup and imaging/biomarkers as an emerging biomarker with modest current discrimination.

### 4) TULSA focal/whole-gland ablation review: low major-complication rates in published studies, but residual cancer and salvage evidence remain important

- **Citation:** Nadeem A, Javed D, Iqbal T, Klair AN, Nadeem F, Nadeem M, Sardar MB. **Magnetic Resonance Imaging-Guided Transurethral Ultrasound Ablation for Localized Prostate Cancer: A GRADE-Assessed Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.** *Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiology.* Published 2026-09-09. DOI: [10.1007/s00270-026-04561-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00270-026-04561-w). PMID: [42717106](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42717106/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis; 13 studies, 650 patients undergoing MRI-guided transurethral ultrasound ablation (TULSA) for localized prostate cancer in treatment-naïve or salvage settings.
- **Category/categories:** Localized treatment; focal/partial-gland therapy; side effects & quality of life; treatment selection.
- **Rubric score:** 8.2/10.
- **Summary:** Technical success was high and major complications were estimated at about 2%. Continence preservation was substantially better in treatment-naïve patients than salvage patients. However, pooled estimates included biochemical recurrence, clinically significant cancer on follow-up biopsy, and progression to salvage treatment, with high heterogeneity for several oncologic outcomes.
- **Main finding:** TULSA is increasingly discussable as an ablative option in selected centers, but it remains a selection- and follow-up-intensive approach rather than a proven equivalent to surgery or radiotherapy for all localized prostate cancer.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients considering focal or ablative treatment can ask whether TULSA is being proposed as focal, subtotal, or whole-gland therapy; how MRI and template/targeted biopsy mapping confirm suitability; what follow-up MRI/biopsy schedule is required; and what salvage options remain if clinically significant cancer persists or recurs.
- **Limitations:** Evidence is mostly nonrandomized and heterogeneous, with different treatment intents, patient-selection criteria, follow-up protocols, definitions of recurrence, and salvage settings. Long-term comparative cancer-control data remain limited.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Ablation can still cause urinary, sexual, urethral, rectal, infectious, pain, retention, and retreatment complications. Avoid assuming “minimally invasive” means low consequence or low monitoring burden.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to localized treatment and focal-therapy discussion as a TULSA-specific evidence update.

### 5) Urinary proteome biomarkers may behave differently by ancestry; biomarker development needs equity controls

- **Citation:** Ha A, Arbet J, Qiu Z, Khoo A, Main BP, Govindarajan M, Waas M, Liu SK, et al. **Ancestry-Dependent Immunologic and Prognostic Effects Characterize the Prostate Cancer Urinary Proteome.** *Journal of the National Cancer Institute.* Published 2026-09-11. DOI: [10.1093/jnci/djag329](https://doi.org/10.1093/jnci/djag329). PMID: [42730607](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42730607/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Urinary proteomics study; 329 localized prostate-cancer patients, including 135 self-identifying as White and 194 self-identifying as Black, with analyses controlling for age, PSA, and clinical ISUP grade.
- **Category/categories:** Imaging & biomarkers; screening/early detection; diagnostic workup; risk stratification; health equity.
- **Rubric score:** 8.0/10.
- **Summary:** The study identified 110 urinary proteins that differed between groups after adjustment. Immune pathways were particularly dysregulated, and the urinary proteome of Black patients showed more features associated with higher-grade cancers even when grade and PSA were matched. The authors highlighted the importance of controlling for race- and ancestry-associated differences in urinary biomarker development.
- **Main finding:** Biomarker tests can be biased or miscalibrated if ancestry, population mix, diet, environment, access, and tumour biology are not accounted for.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients can ask whether a urine biomarker was validated in people with similar ancestry, PSA range, MRI findings, grade group, comorbidities, and healthcare setting, and whether the result should change biopsy or surveillance decisions.
- **Limitations:** Observational biomarker study; self-identified race is not the same as genetic ancestry and also reflects social/environmental factors. This study does not validate a specific clinical test threshold for individual use.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Do not treat a biomarker result as universally calibrated. Clinician interpretation should consider ancestry, family history, genetic risk, PSA density, MRI quality, biopsy findings, and access-to-care context.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to imaging/biomarkers and diagnostic workup as an equity/validation caution.

### 6) PSMA PET tumour volume in “nonmetastatic” CRPC may identify higher-risk disease invisible to conventional imaging

- **Citation:** Civan C, Karpinski MJ, Darr C, Selvamoorthy T, Rennau LM, Hoberück S, Miederer M, Hölscher T, et al. **PSMA-PET Tumor Volume Stratifies Nonmetastatic Castration-resistant Prostate Cancer.** *European Urology Focus.* Published 2026-09-09. DOI: [10.1016/j.euf.2026.06.006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euf.2026.06.006). PMID: [42716860](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42716860/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Large international multicenter retrospective PROMISE-PET Registry analysis; 514 patients with nonmetastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (nmCRPC) by conventional imaging who underwent PSMA PET between 2013 and 2022.
- **Category/categories:** Advanced/recurrent disease; PSMA PET; staging; systemic therapy planning.
- **Rubric score:** 8.0/10.
- **Summary:** PSMA PET detected metastatic disease in 66% of patients classified as nmCRPC by conventional imaging. Higher PSMA PET tumour volume and PSMA PET-detected distant metastases were associated with worse overall survival.
- **Main finding:** The label “nonmetastatic CRPC” depends on the imaging method used; PSMA PET can reveal disease burden with prognostic meaning, but how best to act on that information still requires evidence and guideline-context.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients with rising PSA despite castrate testosterone and negative conventional imaging can ask whether PSMA PET is appropriate, how results would change treatment or clinical-trial eligibility, and whether treatment decisions are based on conventional-imaging trial evidence or PSMA-era staging.
- **Limitations:** Retrospective registry design, long accrual period, variation in tracers/scanners/treatments, and potential selection bias. Prognostic imaging association does not automatically prove that PSMA PET-guided treatment changes improve survival.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** PSMA PET findings should be reviewed by urology, medical oncology, radiation oncology, and nuclear medicine where relevant. Imaging-driven escalation or metastasis-directed treatment should not be assumed beneficial without considering symptoms, comorbidities, prior therapy, trial evidence, and patient goals.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to advanced/recurrent disease and imaging/biomarkers as a PSMA-era staging caveat.

### 7) ADT-induced hot flashes/night sweats are under-standardized but can materially affect quality of life

- **Citation:** Ajmera M, Lou KJ, Ng S, Helbing M. **Burden of Treatment-Induced Vasomotor Symptoms in Men With Prostate Cancer on Androgen-Deprivation Therapy: A Systematic Literature Review.** *Prostate Cancer.* Published 2026-09-10. DOI: [10.1155/proc/7791468](https://doi.org/10.1155/proc/7791468). PMID: [42724429](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42724429/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Systematic literature review; 52 studies on patient-reported outcome instruments, epidemiology, and humanistic burden of vasomotor symptoms in men with prostate cancer receiving ADT.
- **Category/categories:** Side effects & quality of life; ADT supportive care; fatigue/sleep; sexual health; shared decision-making.
- **Rubric score:** 8.1/10.
- **Summary:** The review found no prostate-cancer-specific patient-reported outcome instrument validated specifically for ADT-induced vasomotor symptom quality-of-life impact. Symptom severity and subjective burden were more closely tied to quality of life than simple frequency. Reported rates varied widely, partly depending on how actively symptoms were monitored.
- **Main finding:** Hot flashes and night sweats should be actively asked about and graded by burden, not dismissed as a minor or inevitable side effect.
- **Patient relevance:** Men starting ADT can ask what to expect, how symptoms will be tracked, which non-drug or medication options are suitable, and how sleep disturbance, fatigue, sexual function, mood, relationship effects, and work disruption will be addressed.
- **Limitations:** Heterogeneous study designs, symptom definitions, monitoring methods, and ADT regimens. The review describes burden and measurement gaps more than it proves one management strategy is best.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Medicines, supplements, acupuncture, behavioral strategies, or cooling/sleep interventions should be reviewed with clinicians, especially with cardiovascular disease, depression medicines, anticoagulants, liver/kidney disease, falls risk, or polypharmacy.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to side-effects/QoL as an ADT symptom-monitoring and supportive-care prompt.

## Overall patient-facing takeaway

This week's strongest themes are **monitoring recovery and symptoms after ADT**, **careful interpretation of PSMA PET and biomarkers**, **equity/ancestry calibration in urine biomarker development**, **cautious assessment of emerging blood biomarkers**, **TULSA/focal-ablation evidence limits**, and **making hot flashes/night sweats part of routine ADT supportive care**. None of these findings is a stand-alone instruction to change screening, imaging, biopsy, medication, surgery, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, focal therapy, testosterone therapy, or survivorship care. They are best used to prepare specific questions for a GP, urologist, radiation oncologist, medical oncologist, nuclear-medicine physician, endocrinologist, pharmacist, pelvic-floor/sexual-health clinician, exercise physiologist, psychologist, specialist nurse, or other qualified clinician depending on context.
