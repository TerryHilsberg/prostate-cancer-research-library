## Accepted items

### 1) MRI-based biopsy risk calculators appear usable across transrectal and transperineal biopsy pathways, but calibration matters

- **Citation:** Zlotta AR, Taborek A, Kwong JCC, Wang A, Boulakia N, et al. **External Validation and Comparison of Magnetic Resonance Imaging-based Risk Calculators for Detection of Clinically Significant Prostate Cancer: A Multi-institutional Study Incorporating Transrectal and Transperineal Biopsy Approaches.** *European Urology Focus.* Published 2026-09-19. DOI: [10.1016/j.euf.2026.06.027](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euf.2026.06.027). PMID: [42763233](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42763233/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Multi-institutional retrospective external-validation cohort; 4,168 Canadian men with pre-biopsy MRI followed by biopsy across academic and community hospitals, including both transrectal and transperineal approaches.
- **Category/categories:** Diagnostic workup; imaging & biomarkers; biopsy decision-making; shared decision-making.
- **Rubric score:** 8.3/10.
- **Summary:** MRI-based calculators performed better than a non-MRI model for detecting clinically significant prostate cancer, but their discrimination was lower than originally reported. Performance was broadly consistent across biopsy route, MRI-fusion technique, PI-RADS version, academic/community setting, age, and ancestry subgroups. Kinnaird, Patel, and Peters models showed better calibration and net benefit in this external dataset.
- **Main finding:** Risk calculators can help structure the “biopsy now versus monitor/clarify first” conversation, but the specific calculator and local calibration still matter.
- **Patient relevance:** A patient with elevated PSA and MRI results can ask which risk calculator is being used, whether it has been validated for the local biopsy route and population, and what risk threshold would actually change the biopsy plan.
- **Limitations:** Retrospective validation; 74% Caucasian ancestry; 43% clinically significant cancer prevalence; models may behave differently in other screening settings, MRI-quality environments, ancestry mixes, PSA ranges, and clinical thresholds.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** A calculator should not override concerning PSA kinetics, abnormal digital rectal exam, high-risk family/genetic history, suspicious MRI, symptoms, or clinician judgment. A low calculated risk is not the same as “no cancer.”
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to diagnostic workup and imaging/biomarkers as a risk-calculator calibration note.

### 2) Pelvic-floor training plus resistance training improved urinary leakage after prostatectomy; device biofeedback was not clearly superior to physiotherapist-guided training

- **Citation:** Babović M, Aguayo D, Wiskemann J, et al. **Non-invasive biofeedback-assisted vs. conventional pelvic floor muscle training combined with high-effort resistance training for urinary incontinence after radical prostatectomy: a randomized controlled trial.** *BMC Urology.* Published 2026-09-14. DOI: [10.1186/s12894-026-02333-9](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12894-026-02333-9). PMID: [42754866](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42754866/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Randomized controlled trial; 109 men after radical prostatectomy for localized prostate cancer, comparing non-invasive biofeedback-assisted pelvic-floor muscle training with conventional physiotherapist-guided pelvic-floor training. Both groups also received 12 weeks of high-effort whole-body resistance training.
- **Category/categories:** Side effects & quality of life; post-prostatectomy urinary incontinence; exercise/rehabilitation; localized treatment survivorship.
- **Rubric score:** 8.1/10.
- **Summary:** Ninety-six participants completed the intervention. Both groups had statistically significant reductions in 24-hour pad weight. Treatment success, defined as at least 50% leakage reduction, occurred in 52.1% overall and did not differ significantly between device-based and physiotherapist-guided training.
- **Main finding:** Structured pelvic-floor rehabilitation combined with supervised resistance training looks useful, but this trial does not prove that a non-invasive biofeedback device is better than skilled physiotherapy.
- **Patient relevance:** Men planning or recovering from prostatectomy can ask about early pelvic-floor assessment, correct technique, progression, whether resistance training is safe for them, and how continence outcomes will be measured beyond “pad count.”
- **Limitations:** Device comparison only; both groups received active exercise support; trial registration was retrospective; results may not apply to men with different surgical timing, baseline leakage, access to physiotherapy, frailty, hernia risk, or other pelvic conditions.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Pelvic-floor and resistance programs should be individualized, especially after recent surgery, with pain, bleeding, urinary retention, pelvic-floor overactivity, hernia risk, cardiovascular disease, bone metastases, falls risk, or other exercise limitations reviewed by clinicians.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to side effects/QoL as a post-prostatectomy rehabilitation prompt.

### 3) Prehabilitation before radical prostatectomy may reduce early incontinence and improve early quality of life, but long-term and erectile-function benefits remain uncertain

- **Citation:** Wan W, Shen L, Chang Y, Jin X. **The impact of prehabilitation on postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis.** *Supportive Care in Cancer.* Published 2026-09-14 / October issue. DOI: [10.1007/s00520-026-11182-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00520-026-11182-z). PMID: [42736496](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42736496/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials; 16 RCTs with 1,542 participants undergoing radical prostatectomy.
- **Category/categories:** Side effects & quality of life; localized treatment; prehabilitation; urinary/sexual outcomes; exercise/rehabilitation.
- **Rubric score:** 8.5/10.
- **Summary:** Prehabilitation was associated with lower urinary-incontinence incidence at 1 month and 6 months after surgery and improved quality of life within 3 and 6 months. The review did not find significant improvement in incontinence severity or erectile function, and 12-month quality-of-life effects attenuated.
- **Main finding:** Preparing before surgery may improve early recovery, especially continence and short-term quality of life, but it should not be oversold as a guaranteed long-term continence or erectile-function solution.
- **Patient relevance:** Before surgery, patients can ask whether pelvic-floor physiotherapy, exercise physiology, nutrition, smoking/alcohol optimization, weight/metabolic support, sexual rehabilitation planning, and psychological preparation should start before the operation rather than after problems appear.
- **Limitations:** Moderate risk of bias overall; varied prehabilitation programs, follow-up time points, surgical techniques, continence definitions, and baseline function. Meta-analysis estimates may not identify which components matter most.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Prehabilitation should be adapted for cardiovascular risk, musculoskeletal limitations, frailty, urinary symptoms, pain, and surgical timelines. Do not delay cancer treatment for a prehabilitation program unless the treating team agrees.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to side effects/QoL and localized-treatment survivorship discussion.

### 4) In a salvage-radiotherapy randomized-trial dataset, equalized trial access did not show worse outcomes for African American men

- **Citation:** Lawal IO, Jani AB, Goyal S, Abiodun-Ojo O, Dhere V, et al. **Effect of demographic characteristics on the outcome of prostate cancer salvage radiotherapy: Analysis from a randomized controlled trial.** *Cancer.* Published 2026-09-15. DOI: [10.1002/cncr.70602](https://doi.org/10.1002/cncr.70602). PMID: [42743317](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42743317/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Secondary analysis from a randomized trial of post-prostatectomy salvage radiotherapy guided by conventional imaging versus 18F-fluciclovine PET/CT; men with detectable PSA after prostatectomy followed for failure-free survival.
- **Category/categories:** Advanced/recurrent disease; salvage radiotherapy; imaging; health equity; shared decision-making.
- **Rubric score:** 8.0/10.
- **Summary:** Across study arms, African American men had higher failure-free survival than men of other races despite lower socioeconomic-status scores and higher allostatic-load scores. The PET-guided arm improved outcomes for both groups, with a smaller between-group difference. The authors interpret this as evidence that access-controlled trial settings can reduce or alter disparities seen in routine care.
- **Main finding:** Race-associated outcome gaps should not be treated as biologically fixed; access, trial participation, imaging availability, treatment quality, and supportive care may substantially shape outcomes.
- **Patient relevance:** Men facing biochemical recurrence after prostatectomy can ask whether salvage radiotherapy decisions include PSA kinetics, pathology, modern imaging availability, treatment timing, comorbidities/metabolic health, and access barriers that could affect care quality.
- **Limitations:** Subgroup/secondary analysis with modest numbers; race categories are imperfect proxies for ancestry, social exposures, access, and structural factors; fluciclovine PET is not the same as PSMA PET; findings should not be used to generalize individual prognosis.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Salvage radiotherapy decisions should consider urinary/bowel/sexual toxicity, prior recovery after surgery, PSA level and doubling time, imaging findings, pathology, ADT use, and multidisciplinary review.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **No major pathway change.** Reinforces equity/access framing for recurrent-disease discussions.

### 5) Men with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer reported high decision satisfaction, but life-impact discussions may need strengthening over time

- **Citation:** Routier Cañigueral P, Wennman-Larsen A, Doveson S, Rönningås U, Holm M, et al. **Satisfaction with treatment decision-making and long-term experiences of life-prolonging treatments for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer.** *BMC Palliative Care.* Published 2026-09-19. DOI: [10.1186/s12904-026-02336-1](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-026-02336-1). PMID: [42760521](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42760521/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Longitudinal observational study; 122 men with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer followed by questionnaires for up to two years after starting life-prolonging treatment lines.
- **Category/categories:** Advanced/recurrent disease; shared decision-making; palliative/supportive care; side effects & quality of life.
- **Rubric score:** 7.8/10.
- **Summary:** Satisfaction with treatment decision-making was generally high at the start of first, second, and third treatment lines. However, scores were lower for discussion of how treatment could affect life situation, and willingness to choose or recommend the same treatment decreased modestly over follow-up.
- **Main finding:** Advanced-treatment decisions need repeated, person-centered conversations about daily life, not only an initial disease-control discussion.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients and carers can ask what each treatment line may mean for fatigue, pain, appointments, scans, blood tests, travel, finances, cognition, mood, sexual function, family roles, emergency risks, and when palliative/supportive-care input should be added.
- **Limitations:** Observational design; 122 participants; setting-specific care pathways; results describe experiences and communication needs rather than proving one decision aid or treatment strategy is superior.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Do not stop or decline systemic therapy based on general QoL concerns alone; discuss goals, symptoms, side-effect management, dose/schedule options, treatment breaks if appropriate, palliative-care support, and emergency red flags with oncology teams.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to shared decision-making as an advanced-disease communication prompt.

### 6) ESUR MRI-AI special report: AI for prostate MRI should stay “human-in-the-loop” until real-world validation and surveillance mature

- **Citation:** Cuocolo R, Ponsiglione A, Agrotis G, Barrett T, Brembilla G, et al. **Current status and future directions of AI in prostate cancer detection on MRI: a special report from the ESUR prostate MRI working group authors.** *European Radiology.* Published 2026-09-18. DOI: [10.1007/s00330-026-12836-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00330-026-12836-6). PMID: [42760428](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42760428/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Expert special report from the ESUR Prostate MRI Working Group; not a new patient trial.
- **Category/categories:** Imaging & biomarkers; diagnostic workup; MRI-first pathways; AI decision support; guidelines/care pathways.
- **Rubric score:** 7.7/10.
- **Summary:** The report argues that prostate MRI AI has promising technical performance but faces barriers to clinical adoption: insufficient real-world validation, human-AI interaction problems, automation bias, legal/accountability concerns, vendor/scanner variability, algorithmic drift, cost-effectiveness uncertainty, and the need for post-market surveillance.
- **Main finding:** AI may help radiologists and pathways, but it should not be treated as an autonomous reader or a substitute for MRI quality, radiologist expertise, biopsy/pathology correlation, and clinical context.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients can ask whether an AI tool was used in their MRI interpretation, whether it is approved/validated locally, how disagreements between AI and radiologist are handled, and whether AI changed the decision about biopsy or surveillance.
- **Limitations:** Expert report rather than outcome trial; recommendations depend on local regulation, implementation, radiologist training, population mix, MRI protocol, and vendor performance.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** AI false reassurance or overcalling could lead to missed clinically significant cancer or unnecessary biopsy. Human review and clear accountability are essential.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to imaging/biomarkers and diagnostic workup as an AI-readiness caution.

### 7) EAU 2026 localized-disease guideline summary reinforces MRI-first diagnosis, targeted plus regional biopsy, PSMA PET staging sensitivity, and selected Grade Group 2 active surveillance

- **Citation:** Cornford P, van den Bergh RCN, Briers E, Chiu P, Eberli D, Epure A, Farolfi A, Fonteyne V, et al. **EAU-EANM-ESTRO-ESUR-ISUP-SIOG Guidelines on Prostate Cancer-2026 Update. Part I: Screening, Diagnosis, and Local Treatment with Curative Intent.** *European Urology.* Published 2026-09-01. DOI: [10.1016/j.eururo.2026.04.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eururo.2026.04.013). PMID: [42457454](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42457454/)
- **Evidence type and population:** Multidisciplinary European guideline summary; literature review covering new English-language evidence from May 2023 through 2025.
- **Category/categories:** Guidelines & care pathways; screening/early detection; diagnostic workup; risk stratification/staging; active surveillance; localized treatment; PSMA PET.
- **Rubric score:** Guideline-watch inclusion; 9.1/10 for source credibility and pathway relevance, despite being outside the strict 14-day publication window.
- **Summary:** The guideline summary supports risk-adapted early detection generally beginning at age 50 and based on individualized life expectancy; multiparametric MRI to reduce unnecessary biopsies; combined targeted and regional biopsy when biopsy is indicated; a new five-tier EAU classification; PSMA PET as the most sensitive method for metastatic spread; active surveillance for low-risk disease and selected favorable intermediate-risk Grade Group 2 lesions; hypofractionated radiotherapy consideration in intermediate-risk disease; and radiotherapy to the primary tumour plus long-term intensified hormonal treatment for cN1 disease.
- **Main finding:** The main patient-facing change is not a single test or treatment: it is a more integrated pathway that links PSA risk, MRI, biopsy targeting, risk classification, PSMA PET staging, and risk-adapted treatment intensity.
- **Patient relevance:** Patients can ask whether their workup follows an MRI-first pathway, whether biopsy plans include targeted plus systematic/regional sampling, whether PSMA PET is appropriate for their risk group, and whether active surveillance is being considered or avoided for clearly stated risk-feature reasons.
- **Limitations:** This is a European guideline summary; local practice, access, reimbursement, and national guidelines may differ. It synthesizes evidence rather than reporting a new randomized trial.
- **Safety/clinical cautions:** Guideline statements are not individualized recommendations. Age, life expectancy, comorbidities, symptoms, PSA kinetics, MRI quality, biopsy pathology, genomic risk, patient values, and treatment availability all require clinician review.
- **Decision-map/category-page change:** **Yes.** Added to guidelines/care pathways and cross-linked categories as a high-level care-pathway reference.

## Overall patient-facing takeaway

This week's strongest themes are **calibrated MRI-based biopsy risk calculators**, **structured pelvic-floor and prehabilitation support around prostatectomy**, **person-centered conversations in advanced disease**, **cautious human-supervised use of MRI AI**, and **EAU 2026 pathway reinforcement for MRI, biopsy, PSMA PET, active surveillance, and local treatment**. None of these findings is a stand-alone instruction to change screening, imaging, biopsy, medication, surgery, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, rehabilitation, focal therapy, or follow-up. They are best used to prepare specific questions for a GP, urologist, radiation oncologist, medical oncologist, nuclear-medicine physician, radiologist, pelvic-floor physiotherapist, exercise physiologist, specialist nurse, palliative/supportive-care clinician, pharmacist, or other qualified clinician depending on context.
