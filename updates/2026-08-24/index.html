    2026-08-24 — Weekly Prostate Cancer Literature & Guideline Scan

    Educational research support only — not medical advice. This update is intended to help patients and caregivers prepare better questions for qualified clinicians. It should not be used to start, stop, delay, or choose screening, diagnostic tests, treatment, medication, supplements, surgery, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, or follow-up without specialist review.

    Weekly scan methods

    • Run date: 2026-08-24 UTC.
    • Window searched: roughly 2026-08-10 to 2026-08-24, emphasizing items newly published or newly indexed in the last 7–14 days.
    • Sources checked: PubMed/NCBI, EAU prostate-cancer guideline page, NCCN public guideline listing, AUA guideline listing, NICE prostate-cancer topic page, ASCO configured source URL, and the repository's configured prostate-cancer source topics.
    • Selection rule: included items scored ≥7.5/10 using the repository selection policy. Preclinical-only studies, single case reports, commercial claims without accessible evidence, protocols without results, and duplicate or low-impact items were excluded.
    • Metadata verification: Accepted PubMed items were checked with NCBI E-utilities for PMID, title, journal, publication date, and article-level DOI where available.

    Accepted items

    1) Negative mpMRI plus negative systematic biopsy: medium-term risk is low, not zero

    • Citation: Aloufi WD, Zhang X, Szewczyk-Bieda M, Nabi G, Wei C, Manfredi L. Long-Term Observed Risk of Clinically Significant Prostate Cancer After Negative mpMRI and Systematic Biopsy in Biopsy-Naïve Men: A MULTIPROS Subanalysis. Prostate Cancer. Published 2026. DOI: 10.1155/proc/9470087. PMID: 42626293
    • Evidence type and population: Post hoc longitudinal subanalysis of the prospective multicenter MULTIPROS trial; 99 biopsy-naïve men with both negative baseline mpMRI (PI-RADS ≤2) and negative systematic biopsy, mean follow-up about 62 months.
    • Category/categories: MRI-first pathways; diagnostic workup; PSA interpretation; biopsy follow-up; shared decision-making.
    • Rubric score: 8.1/10.
    • Summary: Four of 99 men (4.0%) were later diagnosed with clinically significant prostate cancer. Estimated clinically significant cancer-free survival was 100% at 24 months and 97.3% at 60 months, but repeat imaging/biopsy was clinically triggered rather than systematic.
    • Main finding: A negative MRI plus negative systematic biopsy is reassuring in selected biopsy-naïve men, but it is not a permanent rule-out test.
    • Patient relevance: Patients with a negative MRI/biopsy can ask what PSA trend, PSA density, family/genetic risk, DRE change, symptoms, MRI quality, or time interval would trigger repeat MRI or repeat biopsy.
    • Limitations: Small selected subgroup, only four events, post hoc design, and selective rather than protocol-mandated repeat testing may underestimate missed disease.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not treat a negative MRI/biopsy as permission to ignore future PSA changes or symptoms. Follow-up should be individualized with a GP/urologist.
    • Decision-map/category-page change: Yes. Added to diagnostic workup and imaging/biomarkers as a “reassuring but not definitive” follow-up point.

    2) PI-RADS 3 lesions: transition-zone PSA density may refine biopsy decisions

    • Citation: Doğan MA, Saygın H, Asdemir A, Karaca İ, Kır A, Korğalı E, et al. Comparison of Whole-Gland and Transition Zone PSA Density in Predicting Clinically Significant Prostate Cancer in PI-RADS 3 Lesions. The Prostate. Published online 2026-08-23. DOI: 10.1002/pros.70237. PMID: 42633675
    • Evidence type and population: Single-center retrospective cohort; 395 biopsy-naïve men with PI-RADS 3 mpMRI lesions who underwent systematic plus cognitive targeted biopsy.
    • Category/categories: PSA interpretation; MRI-first pathways; biopsy decision-making; diagnostic workup.
    • Rubric score: 7.8/10.
    • Summary: Clinically significant cancer was detected in 22/395 men (5.6%). Whole-gland PSA density and transition-zone PSA density performed better than PSA alone; transition-zone PSA density remained statistically significant in multivariable analysis and could reduce biopsies in this dataset while missing the same number of clinically significant cancers as a PSAD strategy.
    • Main finding: In the PI-RADS 3 “gray zone,” prostate volume compartment measures may help refine risk, but thresholds need external validation.
    • Patient relevance: Patients with PI-RADS 3 findings can ask: What is my PSA density? Is the lesion in the transition zone or peripheral zone? How does prostate size/BPH affect the decision? What is the follow-up plan if biopsy is deferred?
    • Limitations: Retrospective, single-center, small number of clinically significant cancers, cognitive targeting, and threshold findings may not generalize to other MRI protocols or biopsy approaches.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: A density threshold should not be used alone to avoid biopsy when other risk features are concerning.
    • Decision-map/category-page change: Yes. Added to diagnostic workup as another caution against one-size-fits-all PSA thresholds.

    3) Active surveillance: urinary transcript markers plus PSA density and MRI may improve reclassification prediction

    • Citation: Borkowetz A, Gräfe S, Kwe J, Fuessel S, Thomas C, Erdmann K, et al. Combining PSA density and imaging data with transcript markers from urinary cells to improve prediction of prostate cancer reclassification in patients on active surveillance. BJUI Compass. Published 2026 Aug. DOI: 10.1002/bco2.70276. PMID: 42630620
    • Evidence type and population: Prospective urine-sample biomarker study; 74 men on active surveillance sampled before control biopsy.
    • Category/categories: Active surveillance; biomarkers/genomics; MRI; PSA density; risk reclassification.
    • Rubric score: 7.6/10.
    • Summary: PSA density, PSA velocity, and maxPI-RADS each had moderate predictive performance for reclassification. A model combining PSA density, maxPI-RADS, and urinary-cell MCL1 transcript achieved AUC 0.828, with high specificity but modest sensitivity.
    • Main finding: Urine transcript panels may become useful adjuncts for active-surveillance monitoring, especially when combined with MRI and PSA density, but this is not yet a stand-alone monitoring replacement.
    • Patient relevance: Men on surveillance can ask whether any urine/genomic test is validated for their situation, whether it changes biopsy timing, and what false-negative risk remains.
    • Limitations: Small study, limited events, selected transcript panel, and need for independent validation and clinical utility testing.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Biomarker reassurance should not replace scheduled biopsy/MRI/PSA follow-up unless a clinician explicitly explains why it is safe in context.
    • Decision-map/category-page change: Yes. Added to active surveillance and imaging/biomarkers as a validation-dependent monitoring tool.

    4) Nodal staging before prostatectomy: Node-RADS may complement nomograms, not replace them

    • Citation: Sciarra A, Casale P, Bevilacqua G, Basile G, Santarelli V, Buffi NM, et al. Prospective Multicenter Study on the Predictive Role of Node-RADS Score in Patients With Prostate Cancer Who Are Candidates for Radical Prostatectomy: Comparative Analysis with Validated Nomograms and PET/CT Scan. European Urology Focus. Published online 2026-08-21. DOI: 10.1016/j.euf.2026.08.002. PMID: 42629306
    • Evidence type and population: Prospective multicenter study of 267 men undergoing radical prostatectomy with extended pelvic lymph-node dissection; 104 also had PSMA PET/CT.
    • Category/categories: Risk stratification & staging; imaging; localized treatment; surgery planning.
    • Rubric score: 8.0/10.
    • Summary: Briganti 2019 nomogram had the highest standalone AUC (0.711); combining Node-RADS with Briganti 2019 slightly improved AUC to 0.719. Node-RADS had very high specificity but lower sensitivity, while nomograms were better at ruling out nodal disease risk.
    • Main finding: Structured radiology scoring may add useful confirmation of suspicious nodes, but nomograms and pathology-risk variables remain central.
    • Patient relevance: Before surgery or radiotherapy planning, patients can ask how MRI, PSMA PET/CT, nomogram-estimated nodal risk, Grade Group, PSA density, and tumor extent affect lymph-node dissection or radiation-field decisions.
    • Limitations: Selected ePLND-eligible surgical cohort; PSMA PET/CT subgroup was smaller; accuracy was modest and outcome benefit from changing management was not tested.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Pelvic lymph-node dissection and nodal radiation can improve staging or treatment targeting but may add lymphocele, lymphedema, bowel/urinary, nerve/vascular, and fatigue risks.
    • Decision-map/category-page change: Yes. Added to risk stratification/staging.

    5) Salvage prostate re-irradiation with SABR: promising local control but very low-certainty evidence

    • Citation: Gouveia AG, Tao J, Pond G, Dong K, Ye A, Rosa AA, et al. Stereotactic Prostate Cancer Ablative Re-Irradiation: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (SPARE). The Prostate. Published online 2026-08-23. DOI: 10.1002/pros.70233. PMID: 42633701
    • Evidence type and population: PRISMA systematic review/meta-analysis of 30 treatment cohorts from 29 studies, including 1,099 patients receiving SABR re-irradiation for locally recurrent prostate cancer after prior radiation.
    • Category/categories: Recurrent disease; salvage radiotherapy; localized/salvage treatment; urinary/bowel toxicity.
    • Rubric score: 8.2/10.
    • Summary: Pooled 2-year biochemical recurrence-free survival was 62.5%, local relapse-free survival 89.9%, and metastasis-free survival 84.6%. Severe acute GI/GU toxicities and late severe GI/GU toxicities were uncommon in pooled estimates, but heterogeneity was high and evidence certainty was very low.
    • Main finding: SABR re-irradiation is a plausible specialist salvage option for selected local recurrences, but patient selection and long-term safety remain uncertain.
    • Patient relevance: Patients with recurrence after radiotherapy can ask whether recurrence is truly local, how MRI/PSMA PET/biopsy confirm it, and whether options include salvage prostatectomy, brachytherapy, cryotherapy/HIFU, SABR re-irradiation, systemic therapy, or clinical trial/registry enrolment.
    • Limitations: Mostly nonrandomized cohorts, heterogeneous dose/fractionation, follow-up, imaging confirmation, and toxicity reporting; no definitive comparative outcomes.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Re-irradiation can cause serious urinary, rectal, fistula, pain, bleeding, and sexual side effects in some patients. It requires specialist radiation-oncology review.
    • Decision-map/category-page change: Yes. Added to localized treatment and advanced/recurrent disease.

    6) High-volume metastatic hormone-sensitive disease: prostate-directed radiotherapy under doublet therapy remains hypothesis-generating

    • Citation: Koguchi D, Tsumura H, Tabata KI, Satoh T, Shiono Y, Hirano S, et al. Prognostic Impact of the Addition of Prostate-Directed Radiotherapy With or Without Metastasis-Directed Radiotherapy Under Upfront Doublet Therapy for High-Volume Metastatic Hormone-Sensitive Prostate Cancer. The Prostate. Published online 2026-08-23. DOI: 10.1002/pros.70238. PMID: 42633691
    • Evidence type and population: Retrospective study of 239 patients with synchronous high-volume metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer treated with upfront doublet therapy; 32 received prostate-directed radiotherapy with or without metastasis-directed radiotherapy.
    • Category/categories: Advanced disease; metastatic hormone-sensitive disease; radiotherapy; systemic treatment sequencing.
    • Rubric score: 7.6/10.
    • Summary: A 7-month landmark propensity-matched analysis found longer castration-resistance-free survival in the prostate-directed radiotherapy group, but the authors emphasize the findings are hypothesis-generating.
    • Main finding: Local radiotherapy in high-volume mHSPC under modern systemic therapy is not settled; selected patients may be studied, but randomized confirmation is needed.
    • Patient relevance: Men with de novo metastatic disease can ask how disease volume, symptoms from the primary tumor, systemic therapy choice, radiotherapy goals, and trial eligibility affect whether prostate or metastasis-directed radiation is considered.
    • Limitations: Retrospective, small treated subgroup, possible selection and immortal-time/landmark biases, and no definitive overall-survival conclusion.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: This should not be read as a reason to add radiotherapy outside specialist multidisciplinary advice; bowel, urinary, fatigue, and interaction with systemic therapy matter.
    • Decision-map/category-page change: Yes. Added as an advanced-disease watch item, not a practice-changing recommendation.

    7) PSMA PET after focal therapy: useful recurrence localization, but evidence remains single-center

    • Citation: Jafarvand M, Farolfi A, Mei R, Djaileb L, Benz M, Chung A, et al. PSMA PET Imaging for Prostate Cancer Recurrence After Minimally Invasive Focal Therapy: A Single-Center Retrospective Study. Journal of Nuclear Medicine. Published online 2026-08-20. DOI: 10.2967/jnumed.125.271556. PMID: 42624652
    • Evidence type and population: Single-center retrospective study of 112 men with suspected recurrence after focal therapy (HIFU, laser ablation, or cryoablation); subcohort of 39 had MRI and biopsy within 3 months.
    • Category/categories: PSMA PET/imaging; focal therapy; recurrence; localized treatment follow-up.
    • Rubric score: 7.8/10.
    • Summary: PSMA PET/CT showed intraprostatic positivity in 82%, pelvic nodes in 15%, and distant metastases in 9%. In the biopsy/MRI subcohort, per-patient sensitivity was 85% for PET versus 82% for MRI; inter-reader agreement was moderate.
    • Main finding: PSMA PET may help localize recurrence after focal therapy, including possible nodal/distant disease, but it should be integrated with MRI and biopsy rather than used alone.
    • Patient relevance: After focal therapy, patients can ask whether rising PSA or suspicious MRI should lead to MRI, PSMA PET/CT, targeted/systematic biopsy, or a combination before choosing salvage treatment.
    • Limitations: Retrospective, single-center, selected referred population, only 39 patients in the biopsy-correlated subcohort, and moderate reader agreement.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: A scan finding needs clinician interpretation; false positives/negatives can lead to overtreatment or missed recurrence.
    • Decision-map/category-page change: Yes. Added to localized treatment and imaging/biomarkers.

    8) 177Lu-PSMA therapy access: theranostics delays can be a real-world treatment issue

    • Citation: Cerci JJ, Fanti S, Herrmann K, Rodrigues DF, Leite PHF, Masukawa M, et al. The Global Theranostics Access Gap: Real-World Disparities in PSMA PET/CT and 177Lu-PSMA Therapy Between Centers in Brazil and Europe. Journal of Nuclear Medicine. Published online 2026-08-20. DOI: 10.2967/jnumed.126.273029. PMID: 42624649
    • Evidence type and population: Retrospective multicenter access study; 91 patients receiving 177Lu-PSMA therapy in Brazil and Europe.
    • Category/categories: Advanced/recurrent disease; PSMA theranostics; access/equity; care pathways.
    • Rubric score: 7.6/10.
    • Summary: Median time from request to PSMA PET/CT was similar, but time to first 177Lu-PSMA therapy dose was much longer in Brazil than Europe (59 vs 21 days).
    • Main finding: For advanced prostate cancer, access to radioligand treatment is not just a scientific question; supply chains, regulation, referral pathways, and geography can materially affect care timing.
    • Patient relevance: Patients being assessed for radioligand therapy can ask about expected waiting time, referral steps, radiopharmaceutical availability, bridging treatment, symptom monitoring, blood counts, kidney function, salivary-gland effects, and backup options if access is delayed.
    • Limitations: Small, region-specific comparison; not directly an Australian access study; does not show clinical outcome differences caused by the delay.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not infer that delayed or accelerated therapy is best in an individual case; sequencing must account for symptoms, disease pace, prior therapies, marrow/kidney reserve, and clinician judgment.
    • Decision-map/category-page change: Yes. Added as an advanced-disease access/care-pathway point.

    9) ADT bone health: population-management program improved testing and osteoporosis treatment

    • Citation: Shulman MJ, Horton BH, Lo JC, Meganck MJ, Miller MK, Park-Sigal JJ, et al. Standardized Bone Health Assessment and Treatment Among Prostate Cancer Patients on Androgen Deprivation Therapy in a Large Integrated Managed Care Delivery System. Journal of Pharmacy Practice. Published online 2026-08-18. DOI: 10.1177/08971900261478840. PMID: 42613815
    • Evidence type and population: Retrospective health-system study of 9,934 men with prostate cancer receiving at least one ADT dose from 2011–2024; compared patients enrolled vs not enrolled in a bone-health population-management program.
    • Category/categories: Side effects & quality of life; ADT supportive care; bone health; care pathways.
    • Rubric score: 8.0/10.
    • Summary: Enrolled patients had much higher bone-mineral-density testing around ADT initiation (80.6% vs 23.8%) and higher antiresorptive therapy use when indicated.
    • Main finding: System-level prompts and management programs can close important supportive-care gaps for men starting or receiving ADT.
    • Patient relevance: Patients on ADT can ask whether they need baseline and follow-up bone-density testing, vitamin D/calcium assessment, fracture-risk review, resistance/impact exercise advice, fall-risk review, dental review before some bone medicines, and osteoporosis medication when indicated.
    • Limitations: Observational study in one integrated delivery system; process improvements do not directly prove fewer fractures or better survival in this report.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Bone medicines have contraindications and adverse effects, including kidney, calcium, dental/jaw, and atypical-fracture considerations. They should be chosen with clinicians.
    • Decision-map/category-page change: Yes. Added to side-effects/QoL and guidelines/care pathways.

    10) Finasteride long-term safety signal in PCPT: no claims-based evidence for post-finasteride syndrome

    • Citation: Till C, Tangen CM, Goodman P, Unger JM, Thompson IM, et al. No Evidence of Post-Finasteride Syndrome in 267,983 person-years follow-up in the Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial. Urology. Published online 2026-08-21. DOI: 10.1016/j.urology.2026.08.020. PMID: 42628582
    • Evidence type and population: Long-term linkage of Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial participants to Medicare claims; 14,239 men with at least one year of continuous coverage and 267,983 person-years of follow-up.
    • Category/categories: Screening/prevention context; PSA interpretation; medication safety; shared decision-making.
    • Rubric score: 7.8/10.
    • Summary: Across 22 claims-defined conditions potentially attributed to post-finasteride syndrome, none showed a statistically significant association with former finasteride assignment versus placebo.
    • Main finding: This large randomized-trial follow-up does not support a claims-detectable long-term post-finasteride syndrome signal, while it cannot capture every patient-reported symptom nuance.
    • Patient relevance: Men taking or considering finasteride for BPH/hair loss or interpreting PSA while on a 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor can discuss benefits, sexual/mood/cognitive symptom concerns, PSA adjustment, and alternative treatments with clinicians.
    • Limitations: Claims data may miss symptoms not coded for care, PCPT participants were a selected trial population, and results may not generalize to younger hair-loss users or individual adverse experiences.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not start or stop finasteride based on this update. Finasteride can change PSA interpretation and has known potential sexual and other adverse effects that need clinician review.
    • Decision-map/category-page change: Yes. Added to screening/PSA interpretation and shared decision-making cautions.

    Overall patient-facing takeaway

    This week's strongest themes are safer interpretation of negative MRI/biopsy results, more granular PSA-density use in PI-RADS 3 decisions, validation-dependent biomarker monitoring during active surveillance, structured nodal staging before local treatment, cautious salvage options after radiation or focal therapy, and supportive-care systems for ADT bone health and PSMA radioligand access. None of these items is a stand-alone recommendation. They are best used to prepare specific questions for a GP, urologist, radiation oncologist, medical oncologist, radiologist, nuclear-medicine physician, genetic counselor, pelvic-floor/continence clinician, pharmacist, or specialist nurse depending on the clinical context.
