    2026-11-02 — Weekly Prostate Cancer Literature & Guideline Scan

    Educational research support only — not medical advice. Use these notes to prepare questions for a qualified urologist, radiation oncologist, medical oncologist, radiologist, pathologist, GP, specialist nurse, pelvic-floor physiotherapist, dietitian, psychologist, pharmacist, or other relevant clinician.

    Scan scope

    • Run date: 2026-11-02
    • Look-back window: primarily 2026-10-19 to 2026-11-02, prioritizing items published or newly indexed in the last 7–14 days.
    • Sources checked: PubMed/NCBI publication-date and Entrez-date searches; article-level PubMed and Crossref metadata; ASTRO; and public guideline/watch pages for NCCN, EAU, AUA, ASCO, NICE, and Cancer Australia.
    • Selection threshold: accepted items scored ≥7.5/10 under the repository rubric. Recently covered duplicates, preclinical studies, case reports, protocols, narrative reviews without a new decision implication, very small technical series, and commercial claims without accessible evidence were excluded or held for watchlist.

    Accepted items

    1) Updated ASTRO guideline: shorter-course external-beam radiotherapy is now the preferred framework for most localized-disease EBRT discussions

    • Citation: Morgan SC, Halthore A, Jia AY, Kishan AU, et al. Hypofractionated Radiation Therapy for Localized Prostate Cancer: An ASTRO Clinical Practice Guideline. Practical Radiation Oncology. Published 2026-10-27. DOI: 10.1016/j.prro.2026.09.017. PMID: 42946126. ASTRO guideline page.
    • Evidence type and population: Multidisciplinary evidence-based clinical practice guideline for patients with localized N0M0 prostate cancer choosing definitive external-beam radiotherapy; developed with ASCO, AUA, ESTRO, and SUO collaboration/endorsement roles.
    • Categories: Guidelines & care pathways; localized treatment; radiotherapy; side effects & quality of life; shared decision-making.
    • Selection score: 9.5/10 — major specialist-society guideline based on mature randomized evidence, with direct implications for treatment duration, urinary-risk counseling, pelvic-node treatment, and access burden.
    • Summary: This replaces ASTRO's 2018 hypofractionation guideline and gives more definitive recommendations on moderate hypofractionation and five-treatment ultrahypofractionation/SBRT.
    • Main finding: Moderate hypofractionation is recommended over conventional fractionation across all risk groups choosing EBRT. Ultrahypofractionation is a standard option for low-risk patients who decline active surveillance and for intermediate-risk disease; it is conditionally recommended for selected high-/very-high-risk patients. Significant baseline urinary dysfunction favors discussing moderate rather than ultrahypofractionation. Ultrahypofractionated elective pelvic-node treatment and regimens using fewer than five fractions should remain clinical-trial approaches.
    • Patient relevance: A person considering EBRT can now ask why a longer conventional course is being proposed, whether five-treatment SBRT fits the exact risk group and urinary baseline, how seminal vesicles/pelvic nodes will be treated, and what image guidance, motion management, focal boost, and rectal-spacing strategy the centre uses.
    • Limitations: A guideline summarizes population evidence and does not make every fractionation schedule interchangeable. Evidence is less mature for ultrahypofractionation in high-/very-high-risk disease, elective pelvic nodes, unusual anatomy, prior procedures, and substantial baseline urinary symptoms.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Shorter treatment is not automatically safer or appropriate. Dose schedule, ADT indication, urinary obstruction, prostate size/anatomy, prior TURP or other procedures, bowel disease, anticoagulation, pelvic-node indication, image guidance, and centre experience require radiation-oncology review.
    • Repository impact: Updates the localized-treatment, guidelines/care-pathways, and decision-map pages. This materially changes the EBRT schedule-choice branch.

    2) PROTEUS surgery-alone substudy: perioperative apalutamide plus ADT delayed events versus radical prostatectomy alone, but interpretation must remain tied to the blinded parent trial

    • Citation: Briganti A, Steuber T, Shore ND, Gleave M, et al. Treatment Effect of Perioperative (Neoadjuvant and Adjuvant) Apalutamide plus Androgen-deprivation Therapy Versus Radical Prostatectomy Alone in Patients with High-risk Localized or Locally Advanced Prostate Cancer from the PROTEUS Substudy. European Urology. Published online 2026-10. DOI: 10.1016/j.eururo.2026.10.016. PMID: 42966675.
    • Evidence type and population: Open-label randomized substudy; 408 patients with newly diagnosed high-risk localized or locally advanced disease assigned 1:3 to six 28-day cycles of apalutamide plus ADT before and after radical prostatectomy/pelvic lymph-node dissection or surgery alone.
    • Categories: Localized treatment; high-risk/locally advanced disease; surgery; perioperative systemic therapy; PSMA PET endpoints.
    • Selection score: 8.8/10 — randomized, directly decision-relevant evidence, interpreted below the blinded 2,109-patient PROTEUS parent comparison because this smaller substudy was open-label, used nominal secondary analyses, and had nonproportional hazards.
    • Summary: The analysis asks a practical question not answered by the parent placebo-controlled comparison: what happens when perioperative apalutamide plus ADT is compared with radical prostatectomy alone rather than perioperative ADT alone?
    • Main finding: Estimated three-year event-free survival was 63% versus 41% (HR 0.38), and the combined conventional-or-PSMA-PET metastasis-free endpoint favored perioperative treatment (87% versus 78%; nominal HR 0.49). Conventional-imaging metastasis-free survival did not differ significantly (94% versus 92%). Grade 3–4 treatment-emergent adverse events occurred in 31% versus 22%.
    • Patient relevance: For selected surgery-fit patients with high-risk localized or locally advanced disease, perioperative systemic treatment is becoming a real multidisciplinary discussion rather than an assumed nonstandard idea. Patients can ask whether the proposed strategy is guideline-supported and available locally, how their disease matches the trial, and whether surgery, radiotherapy-based multimodal treatment, or a clinical trial is the most appropriate pathway.
    • Limitations: Smaller substudy; open-label 1:3 allocation; median follow-up about 37 months; event-free survival includes biochemical and imaging events rather than overall survival; PSMA PET can increase event detection; conventional-imaging MFS was not significantly different; proportional-hazards assumption was not met; several p values were nominal. The parent PROTEUS trial—not this substudy alone—is the stronger efficacy anchor.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: One year of apalutamide plus ADT adds rash, fatigue, falls/fracture, cardiovascular/metabolic, sexual, bone, drug-interaction, and other monitoring burdens. This does not establish perioperative therapy for every high-risk patient or replace comparison with radiotherapy plus evidence-based systemic therapy.
    • Repository impact: Updates the localized-treatment and decision-map pages. It refines the high-risk surgery branch but does not create a stand-alone recommendation.

    3) Multimodal prehabilitation before robotic prostatectomy improved early patient-reported recovery in a small randomized trial

    • Citation: Carbonell E, Dana F, Tena B, Sebio-García R, et al. A Randomized Trial of Multimodal Prehabilitation Before Robotic-Assisted Radical Prostatectomy to Improve Postoperative Quality of Life and Functional Outcomes. Journal of Urology. Published 2026-10-28. DOI: 10.1097/JU.0000000000005365. PMID: 42949849.
    • Evidence type and population: Prospective single-centre randomized trial; 100 men scheduled for robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy, with 95 analyzed, comparing usual care with four weeks of physical and pelvic-floor training, nutrition counseling, and psychological support.
    • Categories: Localized treatment; surgery; prehabilitation; urinary continence; mental health; quality of life.
    • Selection score: 8.2/10 — randomized patient-centred evidence with a clinically meaningful primary outcome, limited by small size, one centre, and a bundled intervention.
    • Summary: The trial assessed whether structured preparation before surgery could improve early quality of life and functional recovery rather than waiting to begin rehabilitation after surgery.
    • Main finding: At one month, global health status was 15 points higher with prehabilitation, with improvements in several quality-of-life and mental-health domains and a modest urinary-incontinence benefit. No erectile-function or physical-performance advantage was detected. The global-health difference diminished by six months, although selected urinary, mental-health, and quality-of-life benefits persisted.
    • Patient relevance: Patients considering prostatectomy can ask whether pelvic-floor instruction, exercise preparation, nutrition review, and psychological support can begin before surgery, who supervises it, and which outcomes the local program has measured.
    • Limitations: Only 95 analyzed patients; single centre; four-component intervention prevents identifying the active ingredient; participants and local support may not represent routine care; early quality-of-life benefit was larger than the six-month difference; no erectile-function benefit was shown.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Exercise and pelvic-floor programs need adaptation for cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, neurological, continence, nutritional, and frailty issues. Prehabilitation supports preparation and recovery; it does not reduce the need for surgical counseling or postoperative assessment.
    • Repository impact: Updates localized-treatment, side-effects/QoL, and decision-map pages. No cancer-control branch change.

    4) Focal therapy versus prostatectomy: better pooled function but less certain cancer control in observational comparisons

    • Citation: Hinojosa-Gonzalez DE, Slawin B, Torres-Martinez M, Coady PJ, et al. Focal therapy vs radical prostatectomy for localized prostate cancer: a systematic review, meta-analysis, Bayesian network, and trial sequential analysis. International Urology and Nephrology. Published 2026-10-27. DOI: 10.1007/s11255-026-05466-w. PMID: 42946901.
    • Evidence type and population: Systematic review/meta-analysis of 11 prospective and retrospective observational comparisons involving 8,399 patients: 3,901 focal therapy and 4,498 radical prostatectomy.
    • Categories: Localized treatment; focal therapy; radical prostatectomy; urinary continence; erectile function; retreatment/salvage.
    • Selection score: 7.9/10 — large comparative synthesis with directly relevant functional and cancer-control outcomes, downgraded because the underlying evidence was nonrandomized, heterogeneous, and vulnerable to major selection bias.
    • Summary: The review compared the main focal-therapy trade-off with surgery: functional preservation versus confidence in durable cancer control.
    • Main finding: Pooled analyses favored focal therapy for continence and erectile-function preservation, including at 12 months, but favored prostatectomy for salvage-treatment-free and failure-free survival. Overall-survival differences were not statistically significant. Cancer-control differences attenuated in propensity-matched analyses, and trial-sequential analysis remained inconclusive.
    • Patient relevance: This makes the trade-off more explicit: focal therapy may reduce early urinary/sexual harm for well-selected patients, but it carries more uncertainty about residual/out-of-field cancer, repeat biopsy, retreatment, and later radical treatment.
    • Limitations: No randomized focal-therapy-versus-prostatectomy trials in the pooled evidence; treatment groups likely differed in age, risk, tumour burden, anatomy, and follow-up; focal modalities and failure definitions varied; functional outcomes may be measured differently; pooled overall survival is too confounded and immature to prove equivalence.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: The result should not be used to choose focal therapy from functional odds alone. High-quality MRI, targeted plus systematic/template biopsy, risk/stage assessment, lesion location, centre outcomes, mandatory follow-up biopsy, and a feasible salvage plan remain essential.
    • Repository impact: Updates the localized-treatment category and focal-therapy evidence map. No structural decision-map change; strengthens the explicit function-versus-oncologic-certainty trade-off.

    5) Five-alpha-reductase inhibitors and MRI/PSA-density triage: overall MRI performance may be preserved, but evidence is too heterogeneous for a reassuring rule-out shortcut

    • Citation: Harrison G, Toia B, Sahdev A, Rajan P, Lamb BW. The impact of 5-alpha reductase inhibitors on multiparametric MRI diagnostic performance and prostate-specific antigen density triage: A systematic review. BJUI Compass. Published online 2026-10-30. DOI: 10.1002/bco2.70294. PMID: 42966989.
    • Evidence type and population: PRISMA systematic review of seven heterogeneous observational comparative studies, each with at least 100 participants, evaluating mpMRI/PI-RADS, PSA density, or lesion-level ADC in men exposed versus unexposed to finasteride/dutasteride-class medicines.
    • Categories: Screening/early detection; PSA interpretation; MRI-first pathways; biopsy triage; benign-prostatic-hyperplasia medicines.
    • Selection score: 7.7/10 — directly relevant diagnostic synthesis with biopsy-verified clinically significant cancer, but no pooled meta-analysis and only one cohort supporting PSA-density threshold comparisons.
    • Summary: Five-alpha-reductase inhibitors reduce prostate volume and alter PSA interpretation, raising concern that they might also change MRI appearance or invalidate usual PSA-density biopsy thresholds.
    • Main finding: Patient-level clinically significant cancer detection and PI-RADS ≥3 sensitivity were broadly similar between exposed and unexposed men, but one matched lesion-level analysis found lower peripheral-zone conspicuity and lower per-lesion yield. In the single PSA-density cohort, a >0.15 ng/mL/cc trigger missed clinically significant cancer in about 12% of exposed and 11% of unexposed men.
    • Patient relevance: A person taking finasteride or dutasteride should ensure the medicine and duration are visible to the clinician interpreting PSA, prostate volume, PSA density, MRI, and biopsy need. The review does not validate using an unchanged MRI or PSA-density threshold as a stand-alone rule-out.
    • Limitations: Seven observational studies; heterogeneous populations, MRI methods, exposure duration, lesion zones, and thresholds; no quantitative pooling; lesion-level concern was not reproduced broadly; PSA-density evidence came from one cohort.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not stop a 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor or reinterpret PSA by a simple self-applied multiplier without clinician review. PSA trend before and during treatment, prostate volume, DRE, MRI quality, PI-RADS, family/genetic risk, symptoms, infection/inflammation, prior biopsy, and follow-up safety-netting remain relevant.
    • Repository impact: Updates the diagnostic-workup and imaging/biomarker category pages. No decision-map structure change; adds an explicit medication-confounder check before biopsy triage.

    6) TALAPRO-2 biomarker analysis: blood-based tumour burden and early clearance were prognostic, not validated treatment-selection or monitoring rules

    • Citation: Yip SM, Laird AD, Agarwal N, Zschäbitz S, et al. Talazoparib plus enzalutamide versus placebo plus enzalutamide in metastatic androgen pathway modulator-resistant (castration-resistant) prostate cancer (TALAPRO-2): a biomarker analysis of circulating tumour DNA and cells for prognosis and molecular profiling. The Lancet Oncology. Published 2026-10-28. DOI: 10.1016/S1470-2045(26)00355-4. PMID: 42950862.
    • Evidence type and population: Exploratory biomarker analysis nested in the randomized double-blind phase 3 TALAPRO-2 trial; 805 men with asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic first-line metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer.
    • Categories: Advanced disease; liquid biopsy; circulating tumour DNA; circulating tumour cells; HRR testing; PARP-inhibitor therapy.
    • Selection score: 8.2/10 — large phase 3 trial population and clinically relevant molecular/prognostic questions, downgraded because this analysis was exploratory and does not establish biomarker-guided treatment changes.
    • Summary: The analysis compared tissue and ctDNA HRR testing and examined whether baseline ctDNA burden and early ctDNA/CTC changes tracked radiographic progression-free survival.
    • Main finding: Tissue and ctDNA HRR classifications agreed in 84% of patients evaluable by both approaches. Detectable baseline ctDNA identified a poorer-prognosis group, while conversion to undetectable ctDNA and reductions in circulating tumour cells at week 9 were associated with better radiographic progression-free survival in both treatment groups.
    • Patient relevance: Blood-based genomic testing may complement tissue when tissue is unavailable or inadequate, and ctDNA/CTC measures may improve prognosis discussions. Patients can ask what assay was used, whether tissue and liquid results agree, and whether a proposed serial blood test is validated to change treatment rather than merely describe risk.
    • Limitations: Exploratory analyses; not a randomized test of biomarker-directed management; ctDNA detectability depends on tumour burden and shedding; 84% concordance leaves clinically important discordance; prognostic association does not prove that changing therapy when ctDNA remains detectable improves outcomes.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not start, stop, or switch PARP/androgen-receptor therapy from a ctDNA or CTC trend alone. HRR gene, variant classification, germline implications, marrow/kidney function, anemia risk, prior therapy, regulatory access, and oncology/genetics review matter.
    • Repository impact: Updates advanced/recurrent-disease and imaging/biomarker pages. No decision-map change; remains prognostic/assay-complement evidence rather than a validated response-guided pathway.

    7) Primary-staging PSMA PET/CT was associated with better long-term outcomes in a large cohort, but the imaging itself cannot be credited with causing the survival difference

    • Citation: Esmaeilzadeh Asl S, Hoberück S, Sonnenfeld A, Stange T, et al. Impact of PSMA PET/CT Staging on Long-Term Outcomes in Patients with Clinically Localized Prostate Cancer: Results of a Large Single-Center Cohort. Journal of Nuclear Medicine. Published 2026-10-29. DOI: 10.2967/jnumed.126.273261. PMID: 42957210.
    • Evidence type and population: Retrospective single-centre cohort of 6,999 patients diagnosed during 2013–2023; 505 underwent PSMA PET/CT for primary staging. Propensity matching, weighting, landmark, and competing-risk analyses were used.
    • Categories: Risk stratification/staging; PSMA PET/CT; localized high-risk disease; radiotherapy planning; observational outcomes.
    • Selection score: 7.8/10 — large cohort, long institutional experience, multiple bias analyses, and patient-important outcomes, but only a small selected subgroup received PSMA PET and residual confounding remains substantial.
    • Summary: The study examined whether primary-staging PSMA PET/CT was associated not just with more accurate staging, but with biochemical recurrence, cancer-specific survival, and overall survival.
    • Main finding: PSMA PET/CT staging was associated with longer time to biochemical recurrence, cancer-specific survival, and overall survival after adjustment. Associations appeared strongest in high-risk and radiotherapy-treated patients, but the formal interaction by treatment type was not significant.
    • Patient relevance: For high-risk localized disease, the study supports asking whether PSMA PET/CT is likely to change radiation fields, systemic-treatment plans, or the decision between local and metastatic pathways—not merely whether it can find more lesions.
    • Limitations: Retrospective single-centre design; only 7.2% underwent PSMA PET/CT; strong potential for referral, era, access, clinician, treatment, and health-status confounding; association does not prove the scan improved survival; imaging-era changes may travel with other improvements; treatment-modality subgroup findings were exploratory.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: More sensitive staging can cause stage migration and may uncover findings of uncertain significance. PSMA PET should be interpreted with pathology, MRI/conventional imaging, PSA, risk group, symptoms, and multidisciplinary review; it does not by itself dictate treatment escalation or de-escalation.
    • Repository impact: Updates imaging/biomarker and risk-staging watch notes through this weekly page. No decision-map change because causal outcome benefit remains unproven.

    Guideline watch

    • NCCN: The public NCCN cancer-guideline listing now shows Prostate Cancer Version 1.2027 as of 2026-11-02, replacing the 2026 version previously tracked by the repository. A public, prostate-specific change summary and release date were not verified, and the detailed document may require account access. This is therefore an important version watch, not a claim about a specific changed recommendation.
    • ASTRO: The updated hypofractionated-radiotherapy guideline is included above because it is a substantive new final guideline published on 2026-10-27.
    • EAU: The public prostate-cancer guideline remains the 2026 limited update; no new within-window amendment was verified.
    • AUA/SUO: Public prostate-cancer guideline pages continue to show the 2026 amendments already tracked by the repository; no additional within-window prostate-specific amendment was verified. AUA participated in/endorsed the new ASTRO hypofractionation guideline.
    • NICE: The topic page reported no new or updated final prostate-cancer guidance in the preceding six months. The consultation on AI technologies to help detect prostate cancer on MRI remains nonfinal, with consultation closing 2026-11-12.
    • ASCO: No separate new within-window public prostate-cancer recommendation meeting the threshold was verified. ASCO collaborated on the new ASTRO hypofractionation guideline.
    • Cancer Australia / Australian guidance: No new within-window final prostate-cancer diagnostic or treatment guideline was verified; the public Cancer Australia prostate-cancer page remained last updated 2025-07-23. The Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Radiologists endorsed the ASTRO guideline.

    Items excluded or held for watchlist

    • A broad active-surveillance meta-analysis reported reassuring pooled 10-year outcomes and MRI performance, but combined heterogeneous study designs and made stronger comparative claims than the underlying nonrandomized evidence could securely support; recent repository updates already cover MRI/PRECISE and biopsy-deferral safeguards.
    • A meta-analysis of agents added to enzalutamide in unselected metastatic castration-resistant disease found pooled radiographic-progression benefit without clear overall-survival benefit and more toxicity; clinically distinct combinations and biomarker groups made the pooled result less actionable than the TALAPRO-2 biomarker analysis.
    • The 2026 APCCC report was retained as a consensus watch item: only 44 of 134 high-risk/localized/biochemical-recurrence questions reached consensus, and the process did not include a formal systematic review or meta-analysis.
    • A micro-ultrasound learning-curve analysis from OPTIMUM, small prospective ablation/radiotherapy cohorts, early adaptive-radiotherapy series, cost-effectiveness studies, and technical imaging/biomarker reports were held because they did not cross the weekly patient-decision threshold.
    • Preclinical molecular studies, case reports, protocols, bibliometric studies, and small single-centre retrospective technical series were excluded.

    Practical clinician-discussion prompts from this week

    • Radiotherapy schedule: “For my risk group and urinary baseline, why are you recommending conventional, moderate hypofractionated, or five-treatment SBRT, and does pelvic-node treatment change that choice?”
    • High-risk surgery pathway: “Does PROTEUS apply to me, and how does perioperative apalutamide plus ADT around surgery compare with radiotherapy-based multimodal treatment, toxicity, access, and current guidelines?”
    • Prehabilitation: “Can pelvic-floor training, exercise, nutrition, and psychological preparation begin before prostatectomy, and who will supervise and measure recovery?”
    • Focal therapy: “How much functional benefit is realistic at this centre, and what are the in-field/out-of-field recurrence, repeat-biopsy, retreatment, and salvage-radical-treatment rates?”
    • Finasteride/dutasteride: “How has this medicine changed interpretation of my PSA trend, prostate volume, PSA density, MRI appearance, and biopsy threshold?”
    • Liquid biopsy: “Is this ctDNA/CTC result being used as a validated treatment-selection test, a prognostic marker, or an experimental serial-monitoring tool?”
    • PSMA PET staging: “What management decision would change if PSMA PET finds additional disease, and is there evidence that the change improves outcomes for someone like me?”
    • NCCN version: “Has Version 1.2027 changed any recommendation relevant to my exact stage, biomarker profile, imaging, or treatment plan?”

    Medical safety

    These notes are educational research support, not medical advice. Prostate-cancer decisions depend on PSA history and medication effects, MRI and biopsy/pathology, Grade Group, stage, disease volume/timing, symptoms, age, frailty, urinary function, heart/kidney/liver/bone-marrow/bone health, medications and interactions, tumour and inherited biomarkers, quality-of-life priorities, access, and clinician judgment. Discuss diagnosis, biopsy, treatment, surveillance, imaging, medicines, exercise, diet, rehabilitation, supplements, or monitoring changes with qualified clinicians.
