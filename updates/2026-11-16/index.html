    2026-11-16 — Weekly Prostate Cancer Literature & Guideline Scan

    Educational research support only — not medical advice. These notes support discussion with qualified clinicians; they do not recommend starting, stopping, delaying, or changing screening, biopsy, treatment, medicines, or exercise.

    This week's decision-changing finding

    New ACS screening guidance makes age, family history, Black ancestry, health/life expectancy, repeat PSA, MRI/biomarker triage, and avoidance of low-risk overtreatment explicit parts of one pathway. It is US guidance, not an automatic replacement for Australian recommendations. Screening of an asymptomatic person must not be confused with evaluation of symptoms, an already abnormal result, or monitoring diagnosed cancer.[1][10]

    Scan scope and selection

    • Run date: 2026-11-16, Australia/Sydney.
    • Window: 2026-11-02 through the run time on 2026-11-16, emphasizing evidence newly available/indexed since the previous weekly scan.
    • Search: PubMed publication-date and Entrez-date searches for prostatic neoplasms[MeSH Terms] OR prostate cancer[Title/Abstract], with the above date window. Both searches were deduplicated before screening; accepted identifiers were checked against existing repository coverage.
    • Verification: Article-level NCBI abstracts/XML, final PubMed summary records, Crossref title/DOI/date metadata, the ACS patient guidance, PROMISE publisher full text, ClinicalTrials.gov records for TRAP and the exercise study, and public NCCN/EAU/AUA/NICE/Cancer Australia pages. Some publisher full texts were inaccessible; notes do not imply full-text appraisal where only an abstract was available.
    • Access limits: ASCO's configured legacy URL did not resolve and replacement guideline pages returned HTTP 403. NICE's detailed in-development listing was blocked. No absence-of-update claim is made for those inaccessible materials.
    • Rubric: Scores below use quality / patient relevance / recency / pathway applicability / limitations clarity / source credibility, with maxima 3 / 2 / 1 / 2 / 1 / 1. These are editorial selection scores, not certainty ratings or treatment-effect estimates. Every accepted note meets 7.5/10; supporting ACS review evidence is grouped with its guideline rather than double-counted as an independent recommendation.

    Accepted items

    1) ACS 2026: screening is a risk-adapted pathway, not a PSA test in isolation

    • Citation: Hoffman RM, Wolf AMD, Etzioni RD, Oeffinger KC, et al. American Cancer Society guideline for the early detection of prostate cancer, 2026. CA: A Cancer Journal for Clinicians. Online 2026-11-12 (Crossref; Nov–Dec issue). DOI: 10.3322/caac.70112. PMID: 43018083.[1]
    • Supporting citation: Griffin JC, Shiau RL, Troutman CAB, Welsh A, et al. Prostate-specific antigen-based screening for prostate cancer: A systematic review to inform an American Cancer Society guideline. Cancer. Online 2026-11-12; issue 2026-11-15. DOI: 10.1002/cncr.70660. PMID: 43018378. The review includes 29 publications and finds reduced advanced/metastatic diagnosis and variable trial-specific prostate-cancer mortality benefit, alongside false positives, overdiagnosis, biopsy complications, and downstream treatment harms.[2]
    • Evidence/population: Evidence-based US guideline for asymptomatic people considering PSA screening, supported by systematic review and commissioned modeling.[1][2]
    • Categories: Screening/early detection; PSA interpretation; MRI/biopsy; active surveillance; shared decision-making; guidelines.
    • Score: 9.5/10 = 2.8 / 2.0 / 1.0 / 1.8 / 0.9 / 1.0.
    • Summary/main finding: Average-risk men: PSA every two years at ages 50–69. Black men and non-Black men with a strong family history: annually at 45–69. A single first-degree relative diagnosed at ≥65 leads to biennial screening from 45. Ages 70–74 require individualized discussion. Confirmed elevated PSA generally leads to MRI and/or a validated biomarker before deciding on biopsy.[1]
    • Patient relevance: Ask which risk group, local guideline, and follow-up pathway apply. ACS uses evaluation thresholds of PSA ≥3.0 ng/mL at 45–69 and ≥6.5 at 70–74; these are not cancer rule-out thresholds. Routine screening is not recommended from 75 or with life expectancy <10 years; the patient guidance notes exceptional discussions for very healthy men 75–79, particularly with a rising prior PSA. Inherited high-risk variants need a separate discussion.[1][10]
    • Limitations: Trial protocols and mortality findings differ; newer MRI/biomarker pathways have less mature long-term outcome evidence. US age/risk rules are not universally interchangeable with Australian guidance.[2]
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not stop assessment of symptoms, an existing PSA abnormality, or diagnosed cancer because of a screening age/threshold. Review PSA confounders, medicines, previous tests, family/genetic risk, repeat testing and biopsy safety with a clinician; low-risk diagnosis should include active-surveillance discussion.[1][10]
    • Repository impact: Material update to screening, guidelines/care pathways, and the decision map's prediagnosis branch.

    2) Older-age screening: competing illness substantially worsens the modeled harm–benefit balance

    • Citation: Gulati R, Suresh K, Psutka SP, Taylor JMG, et al. Estimated lifetime harms and benefits of continuing biennial prostate cancer screening in older men by comorbidity group. Cancer. Online 2026-11-12; issue 2026-11-15. DOI: 10.1002/cncr.70620. PMID: 43018390.[3]
    • Evidence/population: Two microsimulation models of men screened biennially at 50–70, still undiagnosed at 72, with differing competing-mortality risks; modeled MRI/targeted-biopsy evaluation above PSA 6.5 ng/mL.[3]
    • Categories: Screening; older adults; comorbidity; overdiagnosis; shared decision-making.
    • Score: 8.1/10 = 1.8 / 1.8 / 1.0 / 1.5 / 1.0 / 1.0.
    • Summary/main finding: At age 72, estimated overdiagnoses per cancer death prevented were 2.7–3.1 without comorbidity versus 9.6–10.2 with severe comorbidity. Ratios worsened further by 76, despite modern triage.[3]
    • Patient relevance: The discussion is not simply “am I too old?” It includes health, competing mortality, prior PSA trajectory, tolerance of downstream investigations, and whether discovering a cancer would lead to beneficial treatment.
    • Limitations: Model-based projections, not randomized outcomes or a personal life-expectancy calculator; assumptions about screening history, cancer natural history, MRI/biopsy, treatment and mortality affect results. This does not directly describe never-screened men or those with known cancer.[3]
    • Safety/clinical cautions: A screening-stop discussion must remain separate from clinically indicated diagnostic evaluation or surveillance of established cancer. Ratios are not individual probabilities.
    • Repository impact: Strengthens the screening page's older-age/comorbidity discussion; no separate treatment branch.

    3) Reflex biomarkers can reduce biopsies, but their added value depends on the MRI pathway

    • Citation: Shiau RL, Griffin JC, Troutman CAB, Relevo R, Kondo KK. Biomarker use in the prostate cancer screening pathway: Maps of the evidence. Cancer. Online 2026-11-12; issue 2026-11-15. DOI: 10.1002/cncr.70627. PMID: 43018379.[4]
    • Evidence/population: Evidence maps covering 68 studies of common blood-, urine-, and saliva-based biomarkers after abnormal PSA in asymptomatic adults; searches through October 2025.[4]
    • Categories: Imaging/biomarkers; MRI-first pathways; biopsy triage; screening.
    • Score: 8.2/10 = 2.1 / 1.8 / 1.0 / 1.5 / 0.8 / 1.0.
    • Summary/main finding: Biomarker pathways generally reduced biopsies while maintaining clinically significant cancer detection. Added utility was more plausible after/concurrent with MRI, repeat biopsy, or equivocal MRI; benefit beyond MRI in biopsy-naïve patients was less clear, and few studies compared sequencing.[4]
    • Patient relevance: Ask what decision a proposed test changes, how it adds to PSA density/MRI, and what significant-cancer miss rate and follow-up plan accompany any biopsy deferral.
    • Limitations: Heterogeneous populations, thresholds and comparators; evidence mapping is not a pooled head-to-head ranking of commercial tests, and investigator-reported utility does not establish survival benefit.[4]
    • Safety/clinical cautions: A reassuring biomarker does not independently exclude cancer. Local availability, assay validation, prior biopsy, MRI quality, inherited risk and safety-netting matter.
    • Repository impact: Updates imaging/biomarker category; reinforces adjunctive rather than stand-alone testing in the decision map.

    4) SRAM randomized trial: pelvic-node SBRT had worse patient-reported bowel outcomes despite similar graded toxicity

    • Citation: Poon DM, Wong KC, Lam DC, Chan CF, et al. Toxicity and patient-reported quality of life through 12 months following pelvis-inclusive once-weekly stereotactic body radiotherapy versus conventional IMRT for high-risk prostate cancer: updated results of the SRAM randomized phase II trial. Radiotherapy and Oncology. 2026-11-11. DOI: 10.1016/j.radonc.2026.111870. PMID: 43014569.[5]
    • Evidence/population: Randomized phase II trial; 121 evaluable patients with node-negative high-risk disease. Five once-weekly fractions treated prostate plus elective pelvis versus conventionally fractionated IMRT.[5]
    • Categories: Localized treatment; pelvic radiotherapy; bowel/urinary toxicity; patient-reported outcomes.
    • Score: 9.1/10 = 2.5 / 1.9 / 1.0 / 1.7 / 1.0 / 1.0.
    • Summary/main finding: At 6–12 months, grade ≥2 GI toxicity was 16.7% versus 13.1%, and GU toxicity 11.7% versus 16.4%, neither statistically different. However, EPIC bowel scores were worse after SBRT at nine and twelve months; one SBRT recipient had grade 3 rectal hemorrhage.[5]
    • Patient relevance: Ask about patient-reported bowel bother, not only clinician-graded complication rates, and distinguish prostate-only five-treatment radiotherapy from a plan also treating pelvic nodes.
    • Limitations: Small phase II study, 12-month outcomes and a conventional—not modern moderate-hypofractionated—comparator. Nonsignificance does not prove equivalence or long-term safety; this report does not establish comparative cancer control.[5]
    • Safety/clinical cautions: This does not overturn the previously covered ASTRO trial-only caution for ultrahypofractionated elective pelvic nodes. Bowel/urinary baseline, anatomy, dose constraints, motion control and longer follow-up require specialist review.
    • Repository impact: Updates localized treatment, side-effects/QoL, and the decision map's radiotherapy discussion; no new standard-of-care branch.

    5) Active surveillance: better risk prediction is not proof that routine biopsy can safely disappear

    • Citation: Ahmad B, Masood S, Zarif S. Risk-adapted active surveillance for prostate cancer: Can mpMRI, biomarkers, and genomic classifiers reduce biopsy burden without compromising safety?: A systematic review. Medicine. 2026-11-13. DOI: 10.1097/MD.0000000000051498. PMID: 43024296.[6]
    • Evidence/population: Narrative systematic synthesis of five publications representing four unique studies, one randomized and three observational, with 1,215 participants on surveillance.[6]
    • Categories: Active surveillance; MRI; genomics; repeat biopsy; monitoring safety.
    • Score: 8.2/10 = 1.8 / 1.8 / 1.0 / 1.6 / 1.0 / 1.0.
    • Summary/main finding: MRI, 4Kscore and Decipher improved risk stratification, but long-term safety of routine biopsy omission was not established. A hypothetical 4Kscore strategy could avoid 27% of biopsies but miss two Grade Group 2 upgrades; genomic prediction studies did not themselves test biopsy reduction.[6]
    • Patient relevance: Ask which biopsy is being deferred, why, for how long, and what PSA/MRI/pathology or clinical change triggers reassessment.
    • Limitations: Only four unique studies; heterogeneous and partly noncomparative evidence; linked reports must not be counted as independent trials. Prediction, hypothetical avoidance and proven clinical utility are different endpoints.[6]
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not cancel scheduled monitoring from a stable MRI or low genomic score alone. Histologic reassessment remains part of individualized surveillance when indicated.
    • Repository impact: Updates active-surveillance category and reinforces the decision-map monitoring safeguard; no blanket biopsy-free branch.

    6) Personalized exercise during ADT: promising fatigue improvement, with major attrition and no randomized control

    • Citation: Maughan BL, Gebrael G, Agarwal N, Walker D, et al. A prospective trial of a personalized exercise program in patients with advanced prostate cancer. The Oncologist. 2026-11-12. DOI: 10.1093/oncolo/oyag452. PMID: 43016316.[7]
    • Evidence/population: Prospective single-arm 12-week oncology exercise program; 119 sedentary, fatigued participants with advanced disease on ADT-based therapy, without progression or chemotherapy in the preceding three months. Primary fatigue analysis included 72 participants.[7]
    • Categories: Exercise; ADT; fatigue; fitness; quality of life.
    • Score: 8.0/10 = 1.7 / 1.8 / 1.0 / 1.5 / 1.0 / 1.0.
    • Summary/main finding: Among evaluable participants, PROMIS fatigue improved by 5.1 points (95% CI 3.6–6.7); 76 with paired fitness tests improved peak aerobic capacity by 3.1 mL/kg/min (95% CI 2.1–4.0). Weekly supervised in-person/telemedicine sessions were combined with home aerobic/resistance goals.[7]
    • Patient relevance: Supports asking about an oncology exercise-physiology referral, access by telehealth, fatigue assessment and achievable monitoring goals—not just generic advice to “be active.”
    • Limitations: No concurrent control; only 72/119 contributed primary endpoint data. Selection, missing outcomes, regression to the mean and other care can inflate apparent benefit. No survival or anticancer effect was demonstrated.[7]
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Bone metastases, fracture/fall risk, pain, anemia, cardiac disease, neuropathy and recent treatment require individual exercise adaptation; new or worsening fatigue warrants assessment rather than automatic attribution to ADT.
    • Repository impact: Updates exercise and side-effects/QoL categories; no treatment-selection change.

    7) TRAP blood analysis: ctDNA predicts poorer control after SBRT, but is not yet a treatment-selection rule

    • Citation: Reid A, Tran A, Thakali S, Wetterskog D, et al. Circulating Tumor DNA in Oligoprogressive Prostate Cancer Treated With Stereotactic Radiotherapy: A Prespecified Secondary Analysis of a Nonrandomized Clinical Trial. JAMA Network Open. Retrieved/indexed this week; PubMed lists 2026-11-02, whereas Crossref gives online 2026-11-11. DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.43400. PMID: 43013307. Date discrepancy retained rather than silently harmonized.[8]
    • Evidence/population: Prespecified biomarker analysis within the prospective single-arm phase II TRAP trial; 77 evaluable patients with castration-resistant cancer and ≤2 progressing metastases during androgen-receptor pathway inhibitor therapy; 19 were ctDNA-positive before SBRT.[8]
    • Categories: Advanced/recurrent disease; oligoprogression; SBRT; liquid biopsy.
    • Score: 7.9/10 = 1.9 / 1.8 / 1.0 / 1.4 / 0.8 / 1.0.
    • Summary/main finding: Median progression-free survival was 3.0 months with detectable ctDNA versus 7.8 without; median time to new therapy or death was 15.3 versus 29.6 months.[8]
    • Patient relevance: A few visible progressing lesions may not capture the full biological burden; ask whether blood testing is informative and validated for the proposed management decision.
    • Limitations: Small biomarker subgroups; no no-SBRT randomized comparator and no biomarker-guided randomization. The association cannot prove that ctDNA-negative patients benefit more from SBRT or that positive patients should be denied it.[8]
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Negative ctDNA can reflect low shedding rather than absence of systemic disease. Do not continue, stop or switch systemic therapy based on this assay alone; symptoms, imaging, PSA, prior treatments and oncology review remain necessary.
    • Repository impact: Updates advanced/recurrent and imaging/biomarker categories; remains prognostic evidence, not a new decision rule.

    8) PROMISE registry: PSMA tumour volume predicts survival, but validation was a split of the same registry

    • Citation: Karpinski MJ, Civan C, Hoberück S, Güven O, et al.; PROMISE Registry Group. PSMA Total Tumor Volume: A Simple Quantifiable Biomarker for Overall Survival Prediction—Results from the International PROMISE PET Registry. Journal of Nuclear Medicine. Online 2026-11-12. DOI: 10.2967/jnumed.126.273371. PMID: 43020616.[9][11]
    • Evidence/population: Retrospective international registry of 13,893 patients across disease states; development cohort 9,271 and held-out validation cohort 4,622.[9]
    • Categories: PSMA PET; imaging biomarkers; prognosis; staging/advanced disease.
    • Score: 8.3/10 = 2.1 / 1.8 / 1.0 / 1.5 / 0.9 / 1.0.
    • Summary/main finding: Total PSMA-positive tumour volume predicted overall survival better than PSA alone or mean uptake intensity in the held-out cohort (C-index 0.78 versus 0.71 and 0.62, respectively).[9]
    • Patient relevance: Quantifying burden may improve prognosis discussions beyond “how many spots?” It does not establish which treatment improves an individual's outcome.
    • Limitations: Internal split-sample validation, not an independent prospective treatment trial. No multivariable clinical adjustment in this biomarker comparison; PET timing, scanners and segmentation varied. Strongest discrimination was at high tumour volume; low-volume/localized applicability is less certain. More comprehensive PROMISE models still performed better.[11]
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not apply mixed-stage survival quartiles as a personal forecast, or let automated segmentation dictate escalation. Clinician review and disease-state-specific interpretation are essential.
    • Repository impact: Updates imaging/biomarker category; no treatment-selection or decision-map branch change.

    Guideline watch and corrections

    • ACS: New final 2026 guidance accepted above, not merely a version watch.[1][10]
    • NCCN: Public listing still shows Prostate Cancer Version 1.2027. A new within-window recommendation change was not independently verified from public material; version alone does not prove a change affecting an individual patient.[12]
    • EAU: Public page describes the 2026 limited update of 2025. No new within-window amendment was verified.[13]
    • AUA/SUO: Public guideline directory lists 2026 early-detection and advanced-prostate-cancer guidance; no additional within-window amendment was verified.[14]
    • NICE: Topic page reports zero new/updated guidance in the previous six months and lists development work. Detailed development pages were inaccessible, so no claim is made that a draft became final or that a consultation remains open.[15]
    • Cancer Australia: The fetched prostate-cancer page explicitly says last updated 23 July 2025. This corrects the previous weekly scan's July 2026 label; it is not a new clinical update.[16]
    • ASCO: Access limitation noted above; no independently verified new within-window prostate-specific guideline was accepted.

    Excluded or held for follow-up

    • Biopsy-free prostatectomy meta-analysis (PMID 43007457): 11 selected single-arm studies, serious verification/selection bias, no comparative pathway, and overtreatment risk. High cancer yield in a preselected surgical cohort does not justify routine omission of diagnostic biopsy; below inclusion threshold.
    • PREDICT pelvic-field calculator (PMID 43033911): Development performance and a candidate threshold did not transport convincingly to external cohorts; no common threshold or benefit of tool-guided treatment was validated. Watchlist, not a new pelvic-field rule.
    • Surgery-versus-radiotherapy retrospective comparison (PMID 43013243): Weighting cannot eliminate confounding by health, treatment selection, era or regimen; not accepted as evidence that surgery causes superior survival.
    • REQUITE multidimensional-fatigue companion analysis (PMID 43003921): Relevant but overlapping with the cohort/clinical issue covered on 2026-11-09; not counted as an independent new fatigue-outcomes study.
    • PARP-combination meta-analysis (PMID 43009426): Held for fuller appraisal of biomarker eligibility, regimen mixing, toxicity and trial overlap; not used to infer equal benefit across HRR genes or all-comer eligibility.
    • Small technical/AI cohorts, case reports, protocols, narrative reviews, preclinical studies and unsupported commercial claims were not promoted into accepted clinical evidence. Topics searched without an accepted new item should not be interpreted as lacking relevant established evidence.

    Questions to bring to clinicians

    • Screening: “Which guideline and risk group apply, and are we discussing routine screening, diagnostic evaluation, or follow-up of known cancer?”
    • Older-age decisions: “How do my health, previous PSA results and willingness to undergo biopsy/treatment change the benefit–harm balance?”
    • MRI/biomarkers: “What does this extra test add, what important cancers might be missed if biopsy is deferred, and who follows me up?”
    • Radiotherapy: “Are pelvic nodes included, and what do patients report about bowel bother as well as clinician-graded toxicity?”
    • Surveillance: “Which evidence supports deferring this biopsy, and what triggers histologic reassessment?”
    • Fatigue: “Can I access an individualized oncology exercise program, and have other causes of fatigue been assessed?”
    • Advanced disease: “Is this ctDNA or PSMA-volume result prognostic only, or has changing treatment based on it been shown to help?”

    Medical safety

    Educational research support only, not medical advice. Discuss findings with a qualified clinician. Applicability depends on PSA history/confounders and medicines, MRI quality, pathology/Grade Group, stage, symptoms, inherited/tumour variants, prior treatment, age and frailty, urinary/sexual/bowel baseline, cardiovascular/kidney/liver/bone-marrow/bone health, interactions, access and personal priorities. Tests and treatments can cause false reassurance, overdiagnosis, infection, bleeding, urinary retention, urinary/sexual/bowel harm, fatigue and systemic toxicity. Monitoring and contraindications need individualized review; do not delay assessment of concerning symptoms or change established care from this update alone.
