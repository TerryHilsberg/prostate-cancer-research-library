    2026-10-26 — Weekly Prostate Cancer Literature & Guideline Scan

    Educational research support only — not medical advice. Use these notes to prepare questions for a qualified urologist, radiation oncologist, medical oncologist, radiologist, pathologist, GP, specialist nurse, dentist/oral-medicine clinician, sexual-health clinician, pharmacist, or other relevant clinician.

    Scan scope

    • Run date: 2026-10-26
    • Look-back window: primarily 2026-10-12 to 2026-10-26, prioritizing items published or newly indexed in the last 7–14 days.
    • Sources checked: PubMed/NCBI publication-date and Entrez-date searches; article-level DOI/PubMed metadata; and public guideline/watch pages for NCCN, EAU, AUA, ASCO, NICE, Cancer Australia, and Australian prostate-cancer guidance.
    • Selection threshold: accepted items scored ≥7.5/10 under the repository rubric. Recently covered duplicates, preclinical work, case reports, protocols, narrative reviews without a new decision implication, very small technical series, and commercial claims without accessible evidence were excluded or held for watchlist.

    Accepted items

    1) ENZARAD phase 3: extending enzalutamide through radiotherapy did not improve the primary outcome in high-risk localized/locally advanced disease

    • Citation: Williams S, Sweeney CJ, Thomas H, Mak B, Zhang A, Lim TS, et al. ENZARAD (ANZUP 1303): Phase 3 Trial of Enzalutamide With Androgen Deprivation Therapy and Radiation Therapy in High-Risk Localized or Locally Advanced Prostate Cancer. Journal of Clinical Oncology. Published 2026-10-24. DOI: 10.1200/JCO-26-01794. PMID: 42921729.
    • Evidence type and population: Open-label phase 3 randomized trial; 802 participants receiving definitive prostate radiotherapy and 24 months of ADT, randomized to enzalutamide for 24 months or an older nonsteroidal antiandrogen for 6 months.
    • Categories: Localized treatment; high-risk/locally advanced disease; radiotherapy; treatment intensification; systemic-therapy toxicity.
    • Selection score: 9.2/10 — mature randomized phase 3 evidence with a directly decision-relevant metastasis-free-survival endpoint and 92-month median follow-up.
    • Summary: The trial tested whether replacing short-course older antiandrogen therapy with two years of enzalutamide improved outcomes when both groups received long-term ADT and definitive radiotherapy.
    • Main finding: Eight-year conventional-imaging metastasis-free survival was 74% with enzalutamide versus 72% with the comparator (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.67–1.15; P=0.34). Overall survival was not significantly improved. Grade 3–5 adverse events were reported in 185 participants in each arm. A possible benefit in the subgroup planned for pelvic nodal radiotherapy requires caution because it was a subgroup result rather than the overall trial finding.
    • Patient relevance: For a person with high-risk localized or locally advanced disease considering radiotherapy plus long-term ADT, this argues against assuming that longer enzalutamide intensification automatically adds benefit. It supports asking which systemic intensification strategy has evidence for the exact risk group, nodal status, imaging era, comorbidities, and treatment plan.
    • Limitations: Open-label design; comparator and treatment durations differed; conventional rather than PSMA PET imaging defined the primary endpoint; pelvic-nodal-radiotherapy findings may not generalize and should not override the negative overall result.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: This is not a reason to omit indicated ADT, radiotherapy, or another evidence-based intensification strategy. Treatment choice depends on stage, nodal status, life expectancy, cardiovascular/metabolic health, falls/fatigue risk, interactions, and current guideline/regulatory context.
    • Repository impact: Updates the localized-treatment category page. No structural decision-map change; the negative phase 3 result refines the high-risk radiotherapy intensification branch.

    2) CAPItello-281 update: targeted AKT inhibition showed an encouraging survival signal in PTEN-deficient de novo metastatic hormone-sensitive disease, but the analysis was ad hoc

    • Citation: Fizazi K, Clarke NW, De Santis M, Uemura H, Fay AP, Karadurmus N, et al. Capivasertib plus abiraterone in PTEN-deficient de novo metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer: updated survival analysis from CAPItello-281. Annals of Oncology. Published 2026-10-23. DOI: 10.1016/j.annonc.2026.10.097. PMID: 42918424.
    • Evidence type and population: Updated phase 3 randomized-trial analysis; 1,012 patients with newly diagnosed metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer whose tumours were PTEN-deficient by a prespecified immunohistochemistry definition, all receiving ADT plus abiraterone/prednisone or prednisolone.
    • Categories: Advanced disease; biomarker-selected treatment; genomics/pathology; systemic-therapy intensification.
    • Selection score: 8.7/10 — large biomarker-selected phase 3 population and clinically important endpoints, downgraded because this later overall-survival analysis was ad hoc, descriptive, and not prespecified.
    • Summary: The primary trial had already reported a radiographic progression-free-survival benefit from adding capivasertib. This later data cut assessed survival with 42% overall-survival maturity.
    • Main finding: Overall survival numerically favored capivasertib plus abiraterone (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.68–0.99; nominal P=0.044), with medians of 57.4 versus 46.5 months. Updated radiographic progression-free survival remained consistent with the primary analysis (35.5 versus 26.0 months; HR 0.83).
    • Patient relevance: This makes tumour PTEN testing and the difference between a prognostic marker and a treatment-selection marker more relevant in newly diagnosed metastatic disease. Patients can ask how PTEN deficiency was measured, whether the tested regimen is approved/available, and how much confidence should be placed in the current survival estimate.
    • Limitations: The reported survival analysis was not prespecified and was described as exploratory/descriptive; follow-up remains immature for a definitive overall-survival conclusion; applicability is limited to the trial's specific PTEN-deficient, de novo metastatic population and treatment backbone.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not seek or add targeted therapy based on a pathology label alone. PTEN assay method, diabetes/metabolic status, concomitant medicines, steroid exposure, toxicity monitoring, regulatory access, and multidisciplinary oncology review matter.
    • Repository impact: Updates advanced/recurrent-disease and imaging/biomarker category pages. No decision-map change until mature prespecified survival evidence and practice guidance are available.

    3) ENZAMET biomarker analysis: a locked AI pathology classifier may predict who benefits most from enzalutamide, but it is not ready to select routine treatment

    • Citation: Medina S, Tokuyama N, Putcha V, Pathak T, Fu P, Thomas H, et al. AI-Based Pathology Classifier Predicts Sensitivity to Enzalutamide in Metastatic Hormone-Sensitive Prostate Cancer: A Biomarker Analysis of the ENZAMET Trial. Clinical Cancer Research. Published 2026-10-15. DOI: 10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-26-0871. PMID: 42360803.
    • Evidence type and population: Prespecified biomarker analysis using a previously developed and locked digital-pathology classifier; 492 of 1,125 ENZAMET participants had eligible digitized tumour tissue.
    • Categories: Advanced disease; digital pathology; AI decision support; predictive biomarkers; treatment selection.
    • Selection score: 8.4/10 — uses randomized-trial material, a locked classifier, survival outcomes, and a prespecified interaction analysis, but only a tissue-available subset was analyzed and prospective clinical utility is unproven.
    • Summary: The classifier was tested as a possible predictor of differential survival benefit from enzalutamide versus an older nonsteroidal antiandrogen added to ADT.
    • Main finding: The treatment-by-classifier interaction was statistically significant (P=0.014). Enzalutamide was associated with substantial survival benefit in the classifier-negative group (HR 0.50), while the classifier-positive group showed limited evidence of benefit (HR 1.04). These are subgroup biomarker results, not a validated instruction to withhold treatment.
    • Patient relevance: This is a promising example of routine pathology potentially becoming more informative, but patients should ask whether the model has independent prospective validation, whether the assay is clinically available, and whether using it actually improves outcomes compared with standard clinical and genomic assessment.
    • Limitations: Tissue was available for fewer than half of trial participants; subset representativeness and calibration across laboratories/platforms need assessment; comparator therapy reflects the ENZAMET design; predictive performance needs prospective clinical-utility validation.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: An AI classifier should not be used alone to start, stop, or withhold an androgen-receptor pathway inhibitor. Disease volume/timing, symptoms, comorbidities, other treatment options, pathology quality, and clinician judgment remain essential.
    • Repository impact: Updates imaging/biomarker and advanced-disease watch notes. No decision-map change; remains a validation-dependent predictive-biomarker candidate.

    4) Denosumab duration and jaw osteonecrosis: longer exposure was associated with cumulative harm in a large real-world mCRPC cohort

    • Citation: Kim S, Jung S, Bu S, Kim JW, Pyo SW, Lee GJ, et al. Denosumab Therapy Duration and Osteonecrosis of the Jaw and Skeletal Events in Bone-Metastatic Prostate Cancer. JAMA Network Open. Published 2026-10-01; newly indexed 2026-10-22. DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.39765. PMID: 42905706.
    • Evidence type and population: Nationwide Korean retrospective cohort with target-trial emulation methods; 3,307 men with bone-metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer starting oncology-dose denosumab.
    • Categories: Advanced disease; bone health; medication safety; dental/oral health; quality of life.
    • Selection score: 8.3/10 — large safety-focused real-world analysis with competing-risk methods and clinically important outcomes, but nonrandomized duration comparisons remain vulnerable to confounding.
    • Summary: Investigators compared denosumab exposure-duration strategies and estimated three-year osteonecrosis-of-the-jaw and skeletal-event outcomes.
    • Main finding: Overall three-year cumulative incidence was 9.8% for jaw osteonecrosis and 4.9% for skeletal events. Compared with 7–12 months, treatment for at least 13 months was associated with higher jaw-osteonecrosis incidence (12.20%; risk difference 8.93 percentage points) without a further observed reduction in skeletal events.
    • Patient relevance: This supports explicit review of ongoing benefit, cumulative jaw risk, dental status, dose interval, competing health risks, and treatment goals rather than allowing bone-targeted therapy to continue without periodic reassessment.
    • Limitations: Observational claims data; no randomization of duration; residual confounding and treatment-selection effects; findings apply to oncology-dose treatment in bone-metastatic castration-resistant disease and not automatically to osteoporosis-dose schedules or other disease states.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not stop or delay denosumab without oncology review because interruption can have consequences and skeletal-event risk remains important. Dental assessment, preventive care, communication before invasive dental procedures, and prompt review of jaw pain, exposed bone, nonhealing extraction sites, swelling, or infection are worth discussing.
    • Repository impact: Updates advanced/recurrent-disease and side-effects/QoL category pages. No decision-map change; strengthens scheduled benefit–harm reassessment and dental coordination.

    5) Decision-support interventions: simple booklet-based formats had the clearest signal for reducing treatment decisional conflict

    • Citation: Wen Y, Li Y, Zheng S, Li J, Huang X, Zhang Y, et al. Effects of Decision Support Interventions on Treatment Decision-Making in Patients With Prostate Cancer: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-Analysis. Asia-Pacific Journal of Clinical Oncology. Published 2026-10-16. DOI: 10.1111/ajco.70213. PMID: 42885513.
    • Evidence type and population: Systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis of 13 randomized controlled trials of booklet, digital, and multimodal treatment-decision supports for men with prostate cancer.
    • Categories: Shared decision-making; localized-treatment choices; patient education; health literacy.
    • Selection score: 8.0/10 — randomized evidence synthesis with patient-centered outcomes, limited by study heterogeneity, small networks, and indirect comparisons.
    • Summary: The review compared decision-support formats on decisional conflict, regret, knowledge, readiness, and psychological distress.
    • Main finding: Booklet-based interventions showed the clearest reduction in decisional conflict (SMD −0.98, 95% credible interval −1.60 to −0.24). Decision supports improved readiness overall, but did not show statistically significant effects on decision regret or knowledge in the network analysis.
    • Patient relevance: A low-burden written aid may be as or more useful than a sophisticated app when it is readable, age-friendly, tailored to the actual decision, and used alongside clinician discussion. Patients can ask for an option grid or written comparison to review with family before deciding.
    • Limitations: Only 13 trials; intervention content and timing varied; network estimates combine direct and indirect evidence; treatment settings, health systems, and outcome measures may not be comparable; reduced conflict does not prove a better medical choice.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: A decision aid should not replace pathology/imaging review or specialist counseling. It should show benefits, harms, uncertainty, and the option of additional time or a second opinion where clinically safe.
    • Repository impact: Updates the shared-decision-making category page. No structural decision-map change.

    6) Trial generalizability: many real-world patients with newly metastatic disease would not have met pivotal trial eligibility criteria

    • Citation: Gandaglia G, Pellegrino F, Hijazy A, De Meulder B, Abbott T, Golozar A, et al. Real-world Data to Assess the Applicability of Randomized Controlled Trials in Newly Metastatic Prostate Cancer. European Urology Focus. Published 2026-10-23. DOI: 10.1016/j.euf.2026.08.011. PMID: 42919757.
    • Evidence type and population: Multidatabase observational eligibility study; 32,585 people with newly diagnosed metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer identified from insurance-claims databases and compared with eligibility criteria from 12 randomized trials.
    • Categories: Advanced disease; evidence applicability; comorbidity/frailty; shared decision-making.
    • Selection score: 7.9/10 — very large and directly relevant to treatment counseling, but claims data lack important clinical detail and the study does not compare treatment efficacy.
    • Summary: The study estimated how many routine-care patients would have been ineligible for major metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate-cancer trials.
    • Main finding: Depending on the trial, 8.6% to 55% of real-world patients would have failed at least one eligibility criterion. Common reasons included anemia, heart failure, and renal failure; 18% to 52% of patients receiving a trial-tested drug would have been ineligible for that drug's corresponding trial.
    • Patient relevance: Trial averages may fit less well when a patient has anemia, kidney impairment, heart failure, frailty, or other comorbidity. This supports asking how trial eligibility differs from the individual's health and whether dose, monitoring, supportive care, or treatment choice should be adapted.
    • Limitations: Claims data incompletely capture performance status, laboratory values, disease volume, metastatic timing, pathology, goals, and clinician reasoning; database representativeness varies; trial ineligibility does not prove treatment is ineffective or unsafe.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not reject effective treatment solely because a trial would have excluded a patient. The correct response is individualized benefit–risk assessment and monitoring, not automatic undertreatment.
    • Repository impact: Updates advanced-disease and shared-decision watch notes through this weekly page. No decision-map change.

    7) ENZAMET Australian cost-effectiveness analysis: clinical benefit remained, but value was highly dependent on drug price

    • Citation: Law CK, Stockler MR, Martin AJ, Davis ID, Sweeney CJ, Morton RL. Cost-effectiveness of adding enzalutamide vs standard non-steroidal antiandrogen drugs for patients with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer undergoing standard first-line therapy: a within-trial, secondary end-point analysis of the ENZAMET phase 3, international randomized trial (ANZUP 1304). EClinicalMedicine. Published 2026-10-05; newly indexed 2026-10-17. DOI: 10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.104207. PMID: 42890411.
    • Evidence type and population: Within-trial Australian health-system cost-effectiveness analysis using prospectively collected ENZAMET randomized-trial outcomes, linked Medicare data, and quality-of-life measures over five years.
    • Categories: Advanced disease; health-system value; access; treatment burden; Australian care context.
    • Selection score: 7.8/10 — strong trial-linked data and direct Australian relevance, but economic conclusions are price-, comparator-, time-horizon-, and health-system-dependent.
    • Summary: The analysis estimated health-system costs, life-years, and quality-adjusted life-years when enzalutamide was added to testosterone suppression rather than an older nonsteroidal antiandrogen.
    • Main finding: Enzalutamide added an estimated AUD$127,262 per patient over five years for gains of 0.24 life-years and 0.15 quality-adjusted life-years, producing an ICER of AUD$835,580 per QALY. Results were highly sensitive to acquisition price; the authors estimated that a much lower tablet price would meet an AUD$50,000/QALY threshold.
    • Patient relevance: Cost-effectiveness is not the same as clinical effectiveness or an individual treatment recommendation. For Australian patients, it supports asking about PBS eligibility, out-of-pocket costs, alternatives with comparable evidence, travel/monitoring burden, and whether price changes alter access.
    • Limitations: Five-year horizon; comparator reflects ENZAMET's older antiandrogen control rather than all current intensification choices; 2023 Australian costs may change; funding and pricing arrangements vary; missing data required imputation.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not choose or reject treatment from an ICER alone. Cancer control, side effects, comorbidities, drug interactions, access, and patient priorities require oncology discussion. The analysis also noted slightly more hospitalizations and musculoskeletal-medicine use, reinforcing falls/fracture and bone-health review.
    • Repository impact: Adds an Australian access/value note to advanced-disease shared decision-making. No category-page or decision-map change beyond the weekly record.

    8) Formal onco-sexology consensus: sexual care should start before treatment and continue through follow-up

    • Citation: Huyghe E, Bondil P, Fiard G, Methorst C, Schoentgen N, Burte C. Francophone Expert Consensus on Onco-Sexological Care for Prostate Cancer. French Journal of Urology. Published 2026-10-22. DOI: 10.1016/j.fjurol.2026.103263. PMID: 42914056.
    • Evidence type and population: Formal expert consensus informed by a literature review; 25 international experts assessed 64 statements and validated 58 recommendations with more than 70% consensus.
    • Categories: Sexual health; side effects & quality of life; survivorship; shared decision-making; supportive-care pathways.
    • Selection score: 7.7/10 — lower than randomized evidence but included as a structured guideline/watchlist item addressing a commonly under-served safety and quality-of-life issue.
    • Summary: The consensus covers patient/partner information, standardized screening for sexual dysfunction and distress, treatment, lifestyle and psychosocial support, and service organization across the prostate-cancer pathway.
    • Main finding: The recommended care pathway includes pre-treatment information, patient-reported screening, early post-treatment review, repeated reassessment, and access to multidisciplinary sexual-health care rather than waiting for patients to raise problems unaided.
    • Patient relevance: Patients and partners can ask before treatment what changes are likely, what rehabilitation/support is available, when follow-up will occur, and who coordinates urology, sexual medicine, nursing, pelvic-health, psychological, and relationship support.
    • Limitations: Consensus and expert opinion rather than comparative outcome evidence; developed for Francophone practice; recommendations may require adaptation to local services, culture, relationships, baseline function, treatment type, and patient preferences.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Sexual medicines, devices, injections, hormonal approaches, and supplements can have contraindications and interaction risks. Management should be individualized, especially with cardiovascular disease, anticoagulation, penile anatomy concerns, or treatment complications.
    • Repository impact: Updates side-effects/QoL and shared-decision-making category pages. No structural decision-map change.

    • EAU: The public prostate-cancer guideline remains the 2026 edition already tracked by the repository; no new within-window prostate-specific amendment was verified.
    • AUA/SUO: The public prostate-cancer guideline set continues to show the 2026 amendments already covered in prior updates; no additional within-window amendment was verified.
    • ASCO: No new within-window public prostate-cancer guideline recommendation meeting the threshold was verified. The configured general ASCO page was not fully accessible from this environment, so no update was inferred from inaccessible content.
    • NICE: No new final prostate-cancer guidance was listed in the last six months. The existing consultation on AI technologies to help detect prostate cancer on MRI remains in development and is not final guidance.
    • Cancer Australia / Australian guidance: No new within-window final prostate-cancer treatment or diagnostic guideline update was verified from the public Cancer Australia page. The ENZARAD and ENZAMET economic publications above are Australian-relevant evidence, not guideline changes.
    • Sexual-health guideline watch: The new Francophone onco-sexology consensus was included above because it gives a structured care pathway, while its regional and consensus-based limitations are kept explicit.

    Items excluded or held for watchlist

    • A randomized diet/exercise intervention in 100 men with overweight/obesity on active surveillance produced substantial weight loss but no overall improvement in quality-of-life, urinary, prostate-specific, or anxiety scores; secondary subgroup signals were exploratory and did not meet the weekly threshold for a new treatment decision.
    • A robot-assisted-prostatectomy network meta-analysis found the evidence network too disconnected and very low certainty to isolate most individual surgical-component effects on early continence; it is useful methodology but not a reliable basis for choosing a surgical package.
    • A phase I/II focal salvage high-dose-rate brachytherapy study and a 30-patient single-fraction radiotherapy cohort were held for watchlist because small noncomparative populations and limited follow-up prevent stronger effectiveness conclusions.
    • A retrospective US electronic-health-record comparison associated androgen-receptor inhibitors with longer survival than abiraterone in metastatic hormone-sensitive disease, but residual confounding and conflicting database findings mean it should not be treated as a head-to-head drug-selection result without randomization.
    • Reviews of biomarkers, treatment sequencing, rare histologies, radiomics, and AI prognosis were retained as background unless they added validated prospective decision utility.
    • Preclinical molecular studies, case reports, trial protocols, bibliometric studies, small single-center technical series, and commercial claims without accessible clinical evidence were excluded.

    Practical clinician-discussion prompts from this week

    • High-risk radiotherapy: “Given ENZARAD's negative overall result, which systemic intensification—if any—has evidence for my exact risk group, nodal status, imaging, and health?”
    • PTEN-targeted therapy: “Was PTEN deficiency tested with a validated method, and how mature and prespecified is the evidence that the proposed targeted regimen improves survival?”
    • AI pathology: “Is this classifier independently validated and clinically available, or is it still a research biomarker that should not drive treatment alone?”
    • Denosumab: “What benefit am I still getting, how often will duration and dose interval be reassessed, and what dental prevention or jaw symptoms should trigger review?”
    • Decision support: “Can I have a clear written option comparison showing cancer-control evidence, urinary/sexual/bowel/fatigue effects, treatment burden, and uncertainty?”
    • Trial fit: “Would my anemia, kidney function, heart health, frailty, medicines, or performance status have excluded me from the trial—and how does that change monitoring or expected benefit?”
    • Australian costs/access: “What are the PBS and out-of-pocket implications, and are there clinically appropriate alternatives with different cost or monitoring burdens?”
    • Sexual care: “Can we document baseline function and goals before treatment, include my partner if I want, and arrange early follow-up rather than waiting for problems to become established?”

    Medical safety

    These notes are educational research support, not medical advice. Prostate-cancer decisions depend on PSA history, MRI and biopsy/pathology, Grade Group, stage and disease volume/timing, symptoms, age, frailty, heart/kidney/liver/bone-marrow health, diabetes/metabolic status, dental health, medications and interactions, tumour/genetic biomarkers, sexual and quality-of-life priorities, access, and clinician judgment. Discuss diagnosis, treatment, surveillance, imaging, medicines, dental procedures, supportive care, sexual rehabilitation, exercise, diet, supplements, or monitoring changes with qualified clinicians.
