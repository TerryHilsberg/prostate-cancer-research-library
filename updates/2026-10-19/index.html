    2026-10-19 — Weekly Prostate Cancer Literature & Guideline Scan

    Educational research support only — not medical advice. Use these notes to prepare questions for a qualified urologist, radiation oncologist, medical oncologist, radiologist, GP, specialist nurse, cardio-oncology team, dietitian, pharmacist, or other relevant clinician.

    Scan scope

    • Run date: 2026-10-19
    • Look-back window: primarily 2026-10-05 to 2026-10-19, prioritizing items published or newly indexed in the last 7–14 days.
    • Sources checked: PubMed/NCBI publication-date and Entrez-date searches; DOI/Crossref metadata; and public guideline/watch pages for NCCN, EAU, AUA, ASCO, NICE, and Cancer Australia.
    • Selection threshold: accepted items scored ≥ 7.5/10 under the repository rubric. Preclinical-only work, case reports, protocols, editorials, bibliometric studies, small retrospective technical series, commercial claims, and recently covered duplicates were excluded or held for watchlist.

    Accepted items

    1) SCARLET-1: externally tested AI for prostate MRI detection and screening

    • Citation: Saha A, Bosma JS, Twilt JJ, Ng ABCD, Asif A, Magudia K, et al. Artificial Intelligence for Prostate Cancer Detection and Screening on MRI in Global Populations: The SCARLET-1 Study. Radiology. Published 2026-10. DOI: 10.1148/radiol.262081. PMID: 42867371.
    • Evidence type and population: Large retrospective development and external-validation study; 22,445 MRI examinations from 21,252 men, with external diagnostic and organized-screening datasets spanning 20 cities in 12 countries.
    • Categories: Diagnostic workup; MRI-first pathways; screening/early detection; imaging/biomarkers; AI decision support.
    • Selection score: 8.6/10 — unusually large and geographically broad external validation with clinically significant cancer as the target, but retrospective and not a prospective patient-outcome or workflow trial.
    • Summary: The AI system was assessed for detection of Grade Group ≥2 cancer on MRI and met prespecified criteria for performance comparable to radiology readings in three external datasets.
    • Main finding: External AUCs were 0.86, 0.89, and 0.84. Performance was broadly consistent across age, risk, lesion location, and ethnicity, but varied materially by site and image quality; 28% of sites had AUC below 0.80.
    • Patient relevance: This strengthens the case for AI as a quality-assurance or second-reader tool in MRI pathways, while making local scanner quality, radiologist oversight, and site validation visible patient questions.
    • Limitations: Retrospective design; performance heterogeneity by site and image quality; an algorithm-level result does not prove fewer unnecessary biopsies, fewer missed significant cancers, better long-term outcomes, or safe autonomous use.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: AI output should not override PSA history, PSA density, DRE, MRI quality, radiologist review, biopsy/pathology, family/genetic risk, symptoms, or clinician judgment. A reassuring AI/MRI result is not a permanent cancer rule-out.
    • Repository impact: Updates diagnostic-workup and imaging/biomarker category pages. No decision-map change; reinforces human-supervised, locally validated AI.

    2) FAST randomized trial: frozen-section-guided MRI-targeted biopsy reduced cores in PI-RADS 4–5 pathways

    • Citation: Jahnen M, Schwamborn K, Sauter A, Korn P, Lischweski F, Pachmayr L, et al. Frozen section analysis assisted MRI targeted prostate biopsy — results of the randomized clinical FAST trial. European Journal of Cancer. Published online 2026-10-08. DOI: 10.1016/j.ejca.2026.117772. PMID: 42858622.
    • Evidence type and population: Prospective randomized noninferiority trial; 160 men with PI-RADS ≥4 lesions randomized to standard combined targeted plus systematic biopsy or three targeted cores with immediate frozen-section analysis and systematic biopsy only when frozen section was negative/inconclusive.
    • Categories: Diagnostic workup; MRI-targeted biopsy; biopsy burden; patient-reported procedural outcomes.
    • Selection score: 8.2/10 — randomized pathway evidence with a direct patient-burden endpoint, but single-center/specialized pathology logistics and a selected high-suspicion MRI population limit generalizability.
    • Summary: The experimental pathway used rapid pathology from targeted cores to decide during the procedure whether systematic cores were still needed.
    • Main finding: Clinically significant cancer detection was 97% in the frozen-section arm versus 93% with standard combined biopsy, meeting the prespecified noninferiority criterion. Median cores fell from 15 to 3, with lower reported pain, discomfort, visible blood in urine, and blood in semen.
    • Patient relevance: For a man with a PI-RADS 4–5 lesion, this raises a future pathway question: can real-time pathology safely reduce biopsy-core burden without losing clinically significant cancer detection?
    • Limitations: Only 160 participants; specialized immediate frozen-section expertise and workflow may not be widely available; results do not establish safety for PI-RADS 3, MRI-negative, repeat-biopsy, or active-surveillance populations; long-term undergrading and treatment-selection consequences need follow-up.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: This does not justify independently refusing systematic/perilesional cores. Biopsy strategy depends on MRI lesion, prior biopsy, prostate size, infection/anesthesia considerations, route, local expertise, and the clinical need to detect multifocal or contralateral disease.
    • Repository impact: Updates the diagnostic-workup category. No decision-map change pending multicenter replication and longer follow-up.

    3) STAMPEDE cardiovascular analysis: baseline cardiovascular disease mattered more than treatment arm, and SCORE2 underestimated risk

    • Citation: El-Taji O, Dutey-Magni P, Brown M, Murray M, Amos C, Gilbert D, et al. Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events in Men with High-risk Localized or Metastatic Prostate Cancer Starting Long-term Hormone Therapy in the STAMPEDE Trial. European Urology. Published online 2026-10-06. DOI: 10.1016/j.eururo.2026.09.001. PMID: 42838799.
    • Evidence type and population: Post hoc analysis of linked health records for 6,292 STAMPEDE participants with high-risk nonmetastatic or metastatic disease starting long-term ADT and randomized treatment intensification comparisons.
    • Categories: ADT/systemic therapy safety; cardiovascular outcomes; localized high-risk disease; advanced disease; quality of life.
    • Selection score: 8.5/10 — large randomized-trial population with linked cardiovascular outcomes, but the cardiovascular analysis was post hoc and underrepresented severe cardiovascular comorbidity.
    • Summary: Investigators assessed major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) after intensification with abiraterone/prednisolone, abiraterone plus enzalutamide, docetaxel, zoledronic acid, or prostate radiotherapy, and evaluated the general-population SCORE2 risk tool.
    • Main finding: No statistically significant MACE increase was detected for the tested intensification strategies versus ADT alone. Prior cardiovascular disease was the strongest predictor. SCORE2 had modest discrimination and underestimated absolute risk in this prostate-cancer population.
    • Patient relevance: The result supports proactive cardiovascular history, blood pressure, lipids, glucose/diabetes, exercise capacity, smoking, weight/body composition, and medication review before and during long-term hormone therapy rather than assuming a generic calculator or the cancer drug alone captures risk.
    • Limitations: Post hoc endpoint analysis; incomplete baseline data for some participants; severe cardiovascular disease was underrepresented; absence of a statistically significant treatment-arm difference is not proof of no individual cardiovascular risk.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not stop or avoid indicated ADT/intensification based on this summary. Chest pain, acute breathlessness, fainting, stroke symptoms, or other urgent cardiovascular symptoms require urgent medical assessment. High-risk patients may warrant GP/cardiology/cardio-oncology involvement.
    • Repository impact: Updates advanced/recurrent-disease and side-effects/QoL category pages. No cancer-treatment decision-map change; strengthens baseline and longitudinal cardiovascular-risk assessment.

    4) LUNAR phase 2 update: radioligand therapy before SBRT delayed progression in selected oligorecurrent disease

    • Citation: Kishan AU, Wilhalme H, Felix C, Enke J, Ali FZ, Nabong R, et al. 177Lu-Prostate-specific Membrane Antigen Prior to Stereotactic Ablative Radiotherapy in Oligorecurrent Prostate Cancer: Secondary and Exploratory End Point Results from the Randomized Phase 2 LUNAR Trial. European Urology. Published online 2026-10-12. DOI: 10.1016/j.eururo.2026.09.005. PMID: 42864228.
    • Evidence type and population: Randomized phase 2 trial; 92 patients with hormone-sensitive oligorecurrent prostate cancer and one to five PSMA PET/CT-detected lesions amenable to SBRT, assigned to SBRT alone or two cycles of 177Lu-PNT2002 before SBRT.
    • Categories: Advanced/recurrent disease; PSMA radioligand therapy; metastasis-directed radiotherapy; treatment sequencing.
    • Selection score: 8.7/10 — randomized, clinically relevant progression and hormone-therapy-free outcomes, but small phase 2 trial using PSMA PET-based progression and not yet definitive for survival or routine practice.
    • Summary: Extended follow-up tested whether adding short-course PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy before lesion-directed SBRT alters disease progression.
    • Main finding: At 31.9 months median follow-up, median progression-free survival was 17.6 versus 7.4 months (HR 0.38), and hormone-therapy-free survival also favored the combination (HR 0.57). Several conventional-imaging/castration-resistance analyses were exploratory; no new safety signal was reported.
    • Patient relevance: For carefully selected oligorecurrent patients, the study supports asking whether treatment is intended to delay systemic hormone therapy, whether PSMA PET-only progression is clinically meaningful, and whether the radioligand-plus-SBRT strategy is available only through a trial or specialist program.
    • Limitations: Only 92 participants; phase 2; relatively short follow-up; primary progression definition included new PSMA PET lesions; exploratory endpoints and multiple comparisons; no mature overall-survival proof.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Radioligand therapy adds radiation exposure, blood-count/kidney/salivary monitoring, travel and appointment burden, and uncertain long-term toxicity. It should not be treated as established routine care for all oligorecurrent disease without multidisciplinary specialist review.
    • Repository impact: Updates advanced/recurrent-disease and imaging/biomarker watch notes. No decision-map change until larger confirmatory evidence and clearer practice guidance emerge.

    5) Talazoparib plus enzalutamide in biomarker-unselected high-volume hormone-naive disease: activity, but no clear added benefit and more toxicity

    • Citation: Zacchi F, Castro E, Imbach K, Rodriguez-Vida A, Simonetti S, Mellado B, et al. Talazoparib plus enzalutamide in high-volume metastatic hormone-naive prostate cancer: a randomized phase 2 trial. Nature Medicine. Published 2026-10-14. DOI: 10.1038/s41591-026-04671-5. PMID: 42875559.
    • Evidence type and population: Small randomized phase 2 trial; 54 men with high-volume metastatic hormone-naive prostate cancer, receiving ADT and enzalutamide, randomized after an enzalutamide lead-in to continued enzalutamide or enzalutamide plus talazoparib without selection for homologous-recombination-repair alterations.
    • Categories: Advanced disease; PARP inhibition; biomarker/genomic selection; systemic-therapy safety.
    • Selection score: 8.3/10 — randomized and highly safety-relevant, with an important negative interpretation, but small, imbalanced randomization and PSA response rather than survival as the primary endpoint.
    • Summary: The trial tested whether adding a PARP inhibitor broadly, rather than restricting treatment by a validated genomic selection strategy, improved deep PSA response.
    • Main finding: PSA <0.2 ng/mL at 12 months occurred in 73% with the combination and 64.7% with enzalutamide alone; the trial did not establish clear additional clinical benefit. Grade ≥3 adverse events were more frequent with the combination (68% vs 35%), predominantly anemia, and two fatal therapy-emergent myeloid neoplasms occurred in genetically susceptible patients receiving the combination.
    • Patient relevance: This reinforces asking why a PARP inhibitor is being proposed, what germline/tumor testing supports it, whether benefit is established for that disease state and biomarker group, and how anemia/blood counts and rare marrow toxicity will be monitored.
    • Limitations: Very small phase 2 trial; biomarker-unselected population; no power for definitive between-group efficacy or survival conclusions; two fatal events cannot quantify population risk precisely but are clinically important.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not start, stop, or combine PARP/androgen-receptor therapy from this summary. Genomic context, blood counts, prior therapies, marrow reserve, drug interactions, regulatory approval, and specialist monitoring matter.
    • Repository impact: Updates advanced/recurrent-disease safety and biomarker-selection notes. No decision-map change; argues against assuming biomarker-unselected combination benefit.

    6) Metformin added to ADT: modest metabolic improvements, no proven overall oncologic benefit

    • Citation: Fakhri F, Alkhairi Z, Sarhan T, Imran G, Imran L, Moola R, et al. Metabolic and oncological effects of metformin added to ADT in prostate cancer: systematic review and meta-analysis including STAMPEDE. Prostate Cancer and Prostatic Diseases. Published 2026-10-14. DOI: 10.1038/s41391-026-01165-w. PMID: 42875601.
    • Evidence type and population: Systematic review/meta-analysis of six randomized trials with 2,415 participants receiving ADT across metastatic, locally advanced, and biochemical-recurrence settings; 78% of participants came from STAMPEDE.
    • Categories: ADT supportive care; metabolic health; advanced/recurrent disease; repurposed medicines.
    • Selection score: 8.1/10 — randomized evidence synthesis with direct metabolic endpoints, but oncologic conclusions are dominated by one trial and heterogeneous disease settings.
    • Summary: The review separated metabolic effects from anticancer outcomes rather than treating metformin as a proven prostate-cancer drug.
    • Main finding: At 12 months, metformin produced small average reductions in waist circumference, HbA1c, and fasting glucose; weight and LDL reductions were not statistically significant. STAMPEDE did not show an overall-survival benefit in the unselected population. A high-volume subgroup signal and a small trial's castration-resistance-free-survival result remain hypothesis-generating.
    • Patient relevance: For men on ADT, this supports discussing evidence-based metabolic monitoring and management while avoiding the assumption that metformin improves prostate-cancer survival in people without another clinical indication.
    • Limitations: Mixed disease states; most participants did not have diabetes; oncologic evidence dominated by STAMPEDE; subgroup interaction was not statistically significant; modest average metabolic changes do not guarantee individual benefit.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Metformin can cause gastrointestinal effects and, in selected kidney/liver/acute-illness contexts, rare serious harm. It should not be self-started as an anticancer supplement or substitute for lifestyle, cardiovascular-risk, diabetes, or standard cancer care.
    • Repository impact: Updates advanced/recurrent-disease and side-effects/QoL pages. No decision-map change; strengthens the separation of supportive metabolic effects from unproven anticancer claims.

    7) ARASENS Q-TWiST analysis: more quality-adjusted time without symptoms or severe toxicity, but also more time exposed to toxicity

    • Citation: Maiorano BA, Smith MR, Hussain M, Saad F, Tombal B, Sternberg CN, et al. Analysis of quality-adjusted time without symptoms or toxicity (Q-TWiST) in patients with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer from the ARASENS trial. European Journal of Cancer. Published online 2026-10-02; newly indexed during this scan window. DOI: 10.1016/j.ejca.2026.117764. PMID: 42892235.
    • Evidence type and population: Secondary quality-adjusted survival analysis using 53-month data from the phase 3 ARASENS trial of darolutamide plus ADT/docetaxel versus placebo plus ADT/docetaxel in metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer.
    • Categories: Advanced disease; treatment intensification; side effects/quality of life; shared decision-making.
    • Selection score: 7.9/10 — based on a pivotal randomized trial and explicitly patient-centered, but post hoc/secondary utility modeling rather than a new randomized comparison.
    • Summary: Q-TWiST combines time with severe toxicity, time without symptoms/toxicity, and time after relapse to express the efficacy–tolerability balance.
    • Main finding: The modeled gain was 6.3 quality-adjusted months with darolutamide triplet therapy, driven by longer time without symptoms/toxicity and less time after progression. Longer treatment also produced more absolute time in the grade 3/4 toxicity state.
    • Patient relevance: This provides a better discussion frame than “survival benefit” alone: how much extra time is expected before progression, how much treatment burden/toxicity is likely, and which side effects would be unacceptable to the individual patient?
    • Limitations: Utility weights and state definitions can alter Q-TWiST results; grade 3/4 adverse events do not capture every daily-life burden; trial participants and fit-for-docetaxel patients may differ from frailer real-world patients.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Triplet therapy is not appropriate for every patient. Fitness, disease volume/timing, neuropathy, blood counts, infection risk, falls/fatigue, cardiovascular/metabolic health, drug interactions, goals, and access require oncology review.
    • Repository impact: Updates advanced/recurrent-disease and side-effects/QoL pages. No decision-map change.

    8) Digital supportive care: useful when fitted to patients and clinical services, not when treated as technology alone

    • Citation: Paterson C, Coro D, Morris JN, Turner MR, Perrotta N, Godfrey M, et al. Digital supportive care in prostate cancer: a systematic review. Supportive Care in Cancer. Published 2026-10-18. DOI: 10.1007/s00520-026-11192-x. PMID: 42891837.
    • Evidence type and population: Systematic review of 88 studies representing 29,256 people affected by prostate cancer; narrative synthesis of patient-facing digital supportive-care preferences, engagement, barriers, and implementation factors.
    • Categories: Shared decision-making; supportive care; side effects & quality of life; survivorship; health equity.
    • Selection score: 7.8/10 — large evidence map of patient experience and implementation, but heterogeneous designs and no pooled proof that a specific digital intervention improves clinical outcomes.
    • Summary: Patients often valued convenient, flexible access to decision aids, symptom tools, information, psychosocial support, physical-activity support, and sexual-health resources when technology was connected to clinician support and the care setting.
    • Main finding: Usefulness depended more on fit among patient, technology, and service than technical sophistication. Barriers included device access/compatibility, digital literacy, emotional readiness, language, income, and limited sexual-wellbeing support.
    • Patient relevance: Digital tools may help prepare questions and track symptoms, but patients should ask who monitors entries, what triggers clinical contact, whether alternatives exist for low digital confidence, and how privacy and accessibility are handled.
    • Limitations: Broad heterogeneity; many studies assessed acceptability rather than durable symptom, quality-of-life, hospitalization, or survival outcomes; potential underrepresentation of lower-income and non-English-speaking groups.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: An app or portal should not replace urgent assessment, direct clinician contact, pathology/imaging review, or individualized management. Patients need explicit escalation instructions for severe or rapidly worsening symptoms.
    • Repository impact: Updates side-effects/QoL and shared-decision-support watch notes through this weekly page; no decision-map or dedicated category-page change.

    Items excluded or held for watchlist

    • A second recent TULSA systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID 42864969) was not republished as an accepted item because the 2026-09-14 update already covered a closely overlapping 13-study/650-patient TULSA meta-analysis with the same patient-facing conclusion: promising functional outcomes in selected patients, but heterogeneous nonrandomized evidence and inadequate long-term comparative cancer-control data.
    • A real-world LHRH agonist-versus-antagonist cardiovascular cohort reported results contrary to some prior expectations, but residual confounding and treatment-selection bias make it unsuitable for drug switching or a stronger weekly conclusion without further triangulation.
    • A prospective mpMRI-plus-PSMA PET whole-mount pathology analysis was held for watchlist because only 97 prostatectomy patients were included and segment-level sensitivity was limited despite high specificity.
    • Reviews of MRI screening, active-surveillance AI quantification, radiomics, and emerging precision diagnostics were useful context but did not add stronger decision-changing evidence than the accepted SCARLET-1 and FAST studies.
    • Cost-effectiveness analyses are jurisdiction-, price-, model-, and threshold-dependent; they were not treated as stand-alone evidence for treatment selection.
    • Preclinical molecular studies, case reports, trial protocols, technical dosimetry studies, and small single-center retrospective cohorts were excluded.

    Practical clinician-discussion prompts from this week

    • MRI/AI: “Has any AI used on my prostate MRI been validated on this scanner, at this centre, and in patients like me—and is it a supervised second reader rather than an autonomous rule-out?”
    • Biopsy strategy: “For my PI-RADS score and prior-biopsy history, why are targeted, perilesional, and/or systematic cores planned, and is any validated pathway available to reduce unnecessary cores?”
    • Cardiovascular risk: “Before long-term ADT or treatment intensification, how will we assess and monitor cardiovascular risk beyond a general-population calculator?”
    • Oligorecurrence: “Is radioligand therapy before SBRT established care, a clinical-trial strategy, or an individualized option—and which endpoint are we trying to improve?”
    • PARP inhibitor selection: “What germline or tumour result supports this PARP-inhibitor combination, and how will anemia, blood counts, and rare marrow toxicity be monitored?”
    • Metabolic care: “Is metformin being considered for diabetes/metabolic indications or as an investigational cancer adjunct, and what benefit is realistically expected?”
    • Triplet therapy: “How much progression-free or symptom-free time might treatment add, and what severe and day-to-day toxicities matter most for my health and priorities?”
    • Digital support: “Who reviews symptom entries, what triggers a clinical response, and what non-digital alternatives are available?”

    Medical safety

    These notes are educational research support, not medical advice. Prostate cancer decisions depend on PSA history, MRI quality, biopsy/pathology, Grade Group, stage, disease volume/timing, symptoms, age, frailty, cardiovascular/metabolic health, kidney/liver/bone-marrow function, medications, genomics, values, local expertise, regulatory access, and clinician judgment. Discuss diagnosis, screening, biopsy, treatment, surveillance, imaging, supportive care, medication, diet, exercise, or monitoring changes with qualified clinicians.
