    2026-11-09 — Weekly Prostate Cancer Literature & Guideline Scan

    Educational research support only — not medical advice. Use these notes to prepare questions for a qualified urologist, radiation oncologist, medical oncologist, radiologist, pathologist, GP, specialist nurse, pelvic-floor physiotherapist, exercise physiologist, psychologist, pharmacist, or other relevant clinician.

    Scan scope

    • Run date: 2026-11-09
    • Look-back window: primarily 2026-10-26 to 2026-11-09, with emphasis on publications or newly indexed records from 2026-11-03 onward.
    • Sources checked: PubMed/NCBI publication-date and Entrez-date searches; article-level PubMed records; Crossref DOI metadata; and public NCCN, EAU, AUA, NICE, and Cancer Australia pages.
    • Selection threshold: accepted items scored ≥7.5/10 under the repository rubric. Duplicates, preclinical work, case reports, protocols, narrative reviews, small technical series, and unsupported commercial claims were excluded or held for watchlist.
    • Metadata caution: PubMed summary records temporarily displayed unrelated DOI metadata for several newly indexed papers. The DOI links below were checked against Crossref and the article title/journal; where a mismatch existed, the Crossref-verified DOI is used.

    Accepted items

    1) Real-world MRI performance is strong overall, but centre-to-centre variation limits stand-alone rule-out claims

    • Citation: Bao J, Su M, Tu H, Zhao L, Ji Y, Jin P, et al. Diagnostic performance benchmarks of MRI for prostate cancer: a large-scale cohort study of 33,759 patients. Insights into Imaging. Published 2026-11-04. DOI: 10.1186/s13244-026-02460-9. PMID: 42980945.
    • Evidence type and population: Multicentre observational diagnostic-performance study of 20,429 patients from nine Chinese institutions, compared with a published worldwide dataset of 13,330 patients from 70 studies; routine radiologist PI-RADS reports and clinically significant prostate-cancer outcomes.
    • Categories: Diagnostic workup; MRI-first pathways; biopsy triage; imaging and biomarkers.
    • Selection score: 8.4/10 — very large real-world dataset and direct biopsy-pathway relevance, downgraded because pooled performance does not guarantee local calibration or replace patient-level clinical context.
    • Summary: The study provides practical PI-RADS benchmarks while showing that MRI performance varies substantially among institutions.
    • Main finding: At PI-RADS ≥3, pooled sensitivity was 92.6% and specificity 51.5%; at PI-RADS ≥4, sensitivity was 81.5% and specificity 78.1%. PI-RADS v2.1 generally performed better than earlier versions, but centre-level heterogeneity was substantial.
    • Patient relevance: A patient can ask about MRI quality, radiologist experience, local cancer-detection performance, PSA density, and what safety-netting is used if biopsy is deferred. A negative or equivocal MRI is not a universal rule-out result.
    • Limitations: Observational data; differences in MRI equipment, protocols, radiologists, biopsy verification, case mix, PSA distribution, and clinically significant-cancer definitions; the global comparison was based on a previously published meta-analysis dataset rather than one harmonized prospective cohort.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Biopsy decisions should integrate PSA trend/density, DRE, family and genetic risk, age/health, MRI quality and PI-RADS, prior biopsy, symptoms, and follow-up capacity. Do not self-interpret MRI as excluding cancer.
    • Repository impact: Updates the imaging/biomarker category. It strengthens the local-calibration and safety-netting caution but does not change the decision-map structure.

    2) Robotic prostatectomy was associated with fewer procedure-coded urinary adverse events, but not less measured incontinence

    • Citation: Carlisle MN, Li KD, Abbasi B, Chu PW, Breyer BN. Urinary adverse events after robotic vs. non-robotic radical prostatectomy in Medicare beneficiaries. Translational Andrology and Urology. Published 2026-10-30; newly indexed during this scan window. DOI: 10.21037/tau-2026-0594. PMID: 42997183.
    • Evidence type and population: Retrospective SEER-Medicare cohort of 17,441 beneficiaries aged ≥66 years treated with radical prostatectomy during 2010–2018; 14,769 robotic and 2,672 non-robotic procedures.
    • Categories: Localized treatment; surgery; urinary complications; quality of life.
    • Selection score: 8.0/10 — large population-based comparative cohort with multiple adjusted and propensity analyses, limited by nonrandomized treatment selection and claims-based outcomes.
    • Summary: The analysis separates procedure-requiring urinary complications from patient-reported continence, which are not the same outcome.
    • Main finding: Claims-defined urinary adverse events occurred in 7.7% after robotic versus 15.3% after non-robotic surgery (adjusted HR 0.50), driven mainly by lower bladder-outlet-obstruction coding in the first postoperative year. No meaningful adjusted difference was detected for procedure-requiring urinary incontinence, and the overall association was no longer evident beyond three years.
    • Patient relevance: Surgical-platform labels should not be treated as guarantees of continence. Patients can ask for the surgeon/team's own continence definitions, pad-use outcomes, bladder-neck/stricture rates, nerve-sparing eligibility, volume, and rehabilitation pathway.
    • Limitations: Retrospective claims study; residual confounding by patient, surgeon, hospital, era, and approach selection; non-robotic cases were heterogeneous; claims miss symptom severity, pad use, bother, and many patient-reported outcomes.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: The study does not prove that a robot caused better outcomes or that robotic surgery is preferable for every patient. Cancer risk, anatomy, prior procedures, surgeon experience, nerve-sparing safety, access, and alternatives remain central.
    • Repository impact: Updates localized treatment and side-effects/QoL. No decision-map branch change.

    3) Active surveillance is increasingly used, but claims-based monitoring adherence remained low

    • Citation: Shen J, Katz AJ, Xie Y, Chen RC, Sayan M. Adherence to active surveillance monitoring among Medicare beneficiaries with prostate cancer. Urologic Oncology. Published 2026-11-02; newly indexed during this scan window. DOI: 10.1016/j.urolonc.2026.10.008. PMID: 42971864.
    • Evidence type and population: Population-based SEER-Medicare cohort of 11,597 men aged ≥65 with low-risk or favourable intermediate-risk prostate cancer managed with active surveillance during 2012–2020.
    • Categories: Active surveillance; follow-up safety; PSA and repeat biopsy; care pathways.
    • Selection score: 8.3/10 — large real-world cohort with direct safety relevance, limited by claims-based definitions and older US Medicare applicability.
    • Summary: Choosing active surveillance safely requires a functioning longitudinal monitoring pathway, not simply an initial decision to avoid treatment.
    • Main finding: Under the study's primary PSA-plus-repeat-biopsy definition, 45.3% met monitoring criteria in year 1, 12.5% through year 2, and 10.7% through year 3. Adherence remained low across diagnosis years. Less stringent definitions improved percentages but still showed major follow-up gaps.
    • Patient relevance: A patient considering surveillance can ask for a written schedule, who tracks overdue PSA/MRI/biopsy, how results are communicated, and what findings trigger reassessment or treatment discussion.
    • Limitations: Administrative claims cannot fully identify clinical intent, MRI-based individualized surveillance, outside-system testing, contraindications, patient preferences, life expectancy, or why biopsy was omitted; findings may not generalize beyond older US beneficiaries.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: This does not mean every patient needs identical biopsy timing. Monitoring should be individualized by risk, MRI/pathology, PSA kinetics, health, and guideline context—but unexplained loss to follow-up can undermine surveillance safety.
    • Repository impact: Updates the active-surveillance category and the monitoring-safeguard wording in the decision map.

    4) Long-term fatigue after radiotherapy follows different trajectories and is strongly signalled by baseline fatigue

    • Citation: Heumann P, Rosas JC, Aguado-Barrera ME, Azria D, Benner A, Briers E, et al. Long-term fatigue based on the EORTC QLQ-C30 up to five years after radiotherapy and its trajectories in men diagnosed with non-metastatic prostate cancer in the international REQUITE study. British Journal of Cancer. Published 2026-11-04. DOI: 10.1038/s41416-026-03644-2. PMID: 42982989.
    • Evidence type and population: Prospective international cohort of 1,758 men treated with radiotherapy for nonmetastatic prostate cancer during 2014–2016, with patient-reported fatigue followed for up to five years.
    • Categories: Side effects and quality of life; radiotherapy; fatigue; survivorship.
    • Selection score: 8.5/10 — large prospective patient-reported cohort with long follow-up and actionable supportive-care implications, but observational risk associations rather than an intervention trial.
    • Summary: Fatigue was not one uniform post-treatment experience; baseline symptoms and comorbidity helped identify people more likely to have persistent or increasing burden.
    • Main finding: About 35% experienced clinically important fatigue at least once; 14% reported it at two years. Pre-radiotherapy fatigue was the strongest measured predictor, with diabetes, higher BMI, and age also associated. Four trajectories were identified: low, moderate, elevated then decreasing, and increasing fatigue.
    • Patient relevance: Fatigue, sleep, pain, diabetes/metabolic health, mood, activity, medicines, anemia and other causes are worth assessing before and after radiotherapy rather than waiting for severe persistent symptoms.
    • Limitations: Observational cohort; fatigue is multifactorial; treatment details and ADT exposure may contribute; associations do not prove that modifying a risk factor will prevent fatigue; loss to follow-up and cohort selection may affect trajectory estimates.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: New or worsening fatigue can have cancer-related, treatment-related, cardiovascular, metabolic, endocrine, hematologic, sleep, medication, infection, or mental-health causes. It warrants clinician assessment rather than self-treatment or assumption that radiotherapy is the only cause.
    • Repository impact: Updates side-effects/QoL and the decision map's outcomes-to-track section.

    5) HRR alterations should not be treated as one prognostic category in metastatic castration-resistant disease

    • Citation: Lorente D, Tello-Velasco D, Ovejero-Sanchez M, Jambrina A, Romero-Laorden N, Gonzalez-Ginel I, et al. Impact of BRCA2, BRCA1, and Other Individual Homologous Recombination Repair Alterations on Survival in Metastatic Castration-resistant Prostate Cancer. European Urology Oncology. Published 2026-11-03. DOI: 10.1016/j.euo.2026.09.018. PMID: 42977597.
    • Evidence type and population: Prospective CAPTURE study cohort of 1,010 patients receiving first-line therapy for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer, with tumour and/or germline next-generation sequencing across individual HRR genes.
    • Categories: Advanced/recurrent disease; genomics; HRR testing; prognosis; precision oncology.
    • Selection score: 8.4/10 — large prospective molecular cohort with direct interpretation relevance, but not a randomized treatment-selection analysis and with small numbers for several individual genes.
    • Summary: Prognosis varied across BRCA2, BRCA1, ATM, CHEK2, FANCA, CDK12 and other alterations, arguing against interpreting every HRR-positive result as biologically equivalent.
    • Main finding: BRCA2 alterations occurred in 12% and non-BRCA2 HRR alterations in 19%. BRCA1, FANCA and CDK12 groups had poorer overall survival than HRR-negative disease and outcomes more similar to BRCA2, whereas ATM and CHEK2 outcomes were closer to HRR-negative disease in this cohort.
    • Patient relevance: Patients can ask which exact gene and variant is involved, whether it is germline or somatic, whether the finding is prognostic or predicts benefit from a specific therapy, and whether genetics counseling/family testing is appropriate.
    • Limitations: Observational prognosis study; treatment heterogeneity; low counts for individual rare genes; multiple comparisons; variant type and biallelic status may matter; findings do not directly establish gene-specific PARP-inhibitor benefit.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Do not start, stop, or select PARP or other systemic therapy from an undifferentiated “HRR-positive” label alone. Variant classification, gene, germline implications, prior therapy, blood counts, kidney function, interactions, regulatory indication, and oncology/genetics review matter.
    • Repository impact: Updates advanced/recurrent disease and imaging/biomarkers. No structural decision-map change.

    6) Better communication was associated with treatment receipt and survival, but causality is not established

    • Citation: Wang JH, Song L, Carmichael BDM, Jones SR, Farnan L, Wallace DD, et al. Patient-Provider Communication, Treatment, and Survival in a Population-Based Cohort: The North Carolina-Louisiana Prostate Cancer Project. JCO Oncology Practice. Published 2026-11-06. DOI: 10.1200/OP-26-00308. PMID: 42993921.
    • Evidence type and population: Population-based observational cohort of 2,258 Black and White participants enrolled during 2004–2009, with vital-status follow-up through 2020; patient-provider communication measured across contextual knowledge, content and interpersonal treatment domains.
    • Categories: Shared decision-making; communication; equity; care delivery.
    • Selection score: 7.8/10 — diverse population-based cohort and patient-centred exposure, downgraded for older treatment era, self-reported communication and substantial potential for confounding.
    • Summary: Higher informational-content scores were associated with greater receipt and timeliness of definitive treatment and lower prostate-cancer-specific mortality.
    • Main finding: Per five-point increase in communication-content score, odds of radiation or prostatectomy versus nondefinitive treatment were higher; among prostatectomy recipients, surgery within three months was more likely. Higher content scores were associated with lower prostate-cancer-specific mortality (subdistribution HR 0.78).
    • Patient relevance: Patients can bring a question list, request plain-language explanation of risk and alternatives, ask for written results, involve a support person or interpreter, and check that follow-up responsibilities are clear.
    • Limitations: Observational association cannot show that communication itself caused treatment or survival differences; communication may reflect access, health literacy, disease severity, trust, clinician/system resources, socioeconomic factors, or unmeasured health differences; care dates predate current MRI, genomic and PSMA pathways.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: More treatment is not automatically better, especially for low-risk disease or limited life expectancy. High-quality communication should support informed, preference-sensitive care—not pressure toward definitive treatment.
    • Repository impact: Updates shared decision-making. No decision-map structural change.

    7) Radiation-safety instructions after lutetium-177 PSMA therapy vary substantially between centres

    • Citation: Zahner J, Unterrainer LM, Czernin J, Calais J, Eiber M, Fendler WP, et al. Patient Communication and Radiation Safety in [177Lu]Lu-PSMA Radiopharmaceutical Therapy: International Survey of 102 Theranostic Centers. Journal of Nuclear Medicine. Published 2026-11-05. DOI: 10.2967/jnumed.126.273336. PMID: 42988564.
    • Evidence type and population: International practice survey of 102 theranostic centres in 23 countries providing inpatient or outpatient lutetium-177 PSMA radiopharmaceutical therapy.
    • Categories: Advanced disease; PSMA radioligand therapy; patient communication; radiation safety; care pathways.
    • Selection score: 7.7/10 — credible international safety-practice survey with immediate patient-information relevance, but not an outcomes study or formal guideline.
    • Summary: Core treatment procedures were relatively consistent, while advice about distancing, transport, household contact, hygiene and duration of precautions varied considerably.
    • Main finding: The authors developed a patient-information template from areas of agreement and disagreement, highlighting the absence of harmonized international post-treatment instructions.
    • Patient relevance: Before treatment, patients and carers can request written centre-specific instructions covering travel home, public transport, sleeping arrangements, contact with children or pregnant people, bathroom/laundry hygiene, work, emergencies, radiation documentation and when precautions end.
    • Limitations: Surveyed practice and expert opinion do not prove which precaution schedule is optimal; respondents may not represent every centre; national radiation rules, isotope handling, dose, inpatient/outpatient pathways and household circumstances differ.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: Follow the treating nuclear-medicine/radiation-safety team's instructions and local regulations rather than a generic internet checklist. Clarify contradictions before discharge, especially for shared housing, caregiving, pregnancy/children, travel or work exposure.
    • Repository impact: Updates advanced/recurrent disease, side-effects/QoL and shared decision-making. No cancer-treatment branch change.

    8) MRI-targeted focal radiation boost showed durable control in a small nonrandomized phase II study

    • Citation: Padayachee J, Sanmamed N, Ye XY, Liu ZA, Lee J, Berlin A, et al. MRI-guided HDR brachytherapy or integrated VMAT boost for tumour-targeted dose escalation in localised prostate cancer: long-term results of the TARGET study. Radiotherapy and Oncology. Published 2026-11-06. DOI: 10.1016/j.radonc.2026.111853. PMID: 42994518.
    • Evidence type and population: Prospective nonrandomized phase II study of 80 patients with localized prostate cancer and an MRI-visible intraprostatic lesion, treated with whole-prostate VMAT plus either integrated VMAT boost or a single HDR-brachytherapy boost; median follow-up 4.8 years.
    • Categories: Localized treatment; radiotherapy; focal boost; MRI-guided treatment; quality of life.
    • Selection score: 7.6/10 — prospective long-term lesion-targeted outcomes with patient-reported toxicity, limited by small size, nonrandom allocation, older fractionation and no standard-treatment comparator.
    • Summary: The study tests focal dose escalation within whole-prostate treatment, not focal ablation that leaves the rest of the gland untreated.
    • Main finding: One recurrence occurred within the boost volume; four intraprostatic failures occurred outside it, and three patients developed regional/distant metastases. Five-year biochemical-failure-free survival was 86.9%, with no substantial late-toxicity or health-related-quality-of-life difference between boost cohorts.
    • Patient relevance: For selected intermediate/high-risk radiotherapy candidates, this supports asking whether the dominant MRI lesion is being boosted, what randomized evidence supports the technique, and how tumour coverage and urinary/bowel constraints are balanced.
    • Limitations: Only 80 patients; nonrandomized boost selection; 66 underwent the three-year MRI or MRI-guided biopsy assessment; few events; limited ADT use; no comparison against modern standard whole-gland radiotherapy without focal boost; technique and fractionation may not match current practice.
    • Safety/clinical cautions: This does not establish equivalence between HDR and VMAT boost or prove superiority over standard radiotherapy. Lesion visibility, risk group, extracapsular disease, urinary function, anatomy, dose constraints, image guidance, ADT indication and centre expertise require radiation-oncology review.
    • Repository impact: Updates localized treatment. It reinforces the focal-radiation-boost branch without changing the decision map.

    Items excluded or held for watchlist

    • PRAISE-U implementation commentary and new screening/MRI consensus pieces were retained as watchlist items because they did not provide a new outcomes dataset or final guideline recommendation that displaced stronger evidence above.
    • A small retrospective nerve-sparing-grade study and multiple radiomics/AI studies were held because selection bias, internal validation or technical endpoints limited near-term patient-decision value.
    • Small oligoprogressive/oligometastatic radiotherapy cohorts, pathology-biomarker studies and exploratory treatment-response cohorts were held for confirmation.
    • Preclinical molecular studies, case reports, protocols, bibliometric studies, narrative reviews and commercial/technical claims without comparative clinical evidence were excluded.

    Practical clinician-discussion prompts from this week

    • MRI/biopsy: “How well does this centre's MRI pathway perform, and what PSA-density, risk-factor and follow-up safeguards apply if biopsy is deferred?”
    • Surgery: “What are your own pad-use, continence, bladder-neck/stricture and re-intervention outcomes, rather than only the label ‘robotic’?”
    • Active surveillance: “Can I have a written PSA, MRI and biopsy schedule, and who identifies and contacts patients when monitoring is overdue?”
    • Fatigue: “Can we assess my baseline fatigue, sleep, pain, metabolic health, medicines, mood and other reversible contributors before and after treatment?”
    • Genomics: “Which exact HRR gene and variant is involved, is it germline or somatic, and is the result prognostic, predictive, or both?”
    • Communication: “Can you explain the options, expected benefits/harms and follow-up responsibilities in plain language and provide written results?”
    • Lutetium-177 PSMA: “What written radiation precautions apply to my household, transport, work and travel, and which local rule determines their duration?”
    • Focal radiation boost: “Is the MRI-visible lesion being boosted within whole-gland radiotherapy, and what randomized evidence and toxicity data apply to the proposed technique?”

    Medical safety

    These notes are educational research support, not medical advice. Prostate-cancer decisions depend on PSA history and medication effects, MRI and biopsy/pathology, Grade Group, stage, disease volume and timing, symptoms, age, frailty, urinary/sexual/bowel baseline, heart/kidney/liver/bone-marrow/bone health, medicines and interactions, tumour and inherited biomarkers, quality-of-life priorities, access, and clinician judgment. Discuss diagnosis, biopsy, treatment, surveillance, imaging, medicines, exercise, rehabilitation, supplements, or monitoring changes with qualified clinicians.
